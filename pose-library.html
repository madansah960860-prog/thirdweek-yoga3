Nothing matches all of that at once

Drop the prop filter first, before the other two. A prop tag names the thing that opens a pose up for most people, not a requirement, so a pose tagged for a block is very often practicable without one.

  • Mountain Pose
    Tāḍāsana
    Standing still with weight spread evenly through both feet. The reference point every other standing pose is measured against.
    • Beginner
    • No prop
  • Downward-Facing Dog
    Adho Mukha Śvānāsana
    Hands down, feet down, hips high. Lengthens the backs of the legs and puts real load through the shoulders and wrists.
    • Full guide
    • Beginner
  • Child's Pose
    Bālāsana
    Kneeling, folded forward, forehead resting on something. The shape every class offers as a rest, and almost nobody sets up properly.
    • Full guide
    • Beginner
  • Warrior I
    Vīrabhadrāsana I
    A long stance with the front knee bent, the back foot turned in and both arms overhead. Asks more of the back ankle than most people expect.
    • Intermediate
    • Block helps
  • Warrior II
    Vīrabhadrāsana II
    Feet wide, front knee bent, arms reaching opposite ways at shoulder height. Simple to see and hard to hold.
    • Full guide
    • Beginner
  • Warrior III
    Vīrabhadrāsana III
    Balanced on one leg with the torso and lifted leg roughly parallel to the floor. Far steadier with the hands on a wall or two blocks.
    • Advanced
    • Wall or blocks
  • Tree Pose
    Vṛkṣāsana
    One leg standing, the other foot pressed into the ankle, calf or inner thigh. Start it beside a wall.
    • Full guide
    • Beginner
  • Bridge Pose
    Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana
    On your back, feet planted, hips lifted. Two different poses really: active leg work, or minutes of rest on a block.
    • Full guide
    • Beginner
  • Seated Forward Fold
    Paścimottānāsana
    Legs long, hinging forward from the hip crease. Sit higher than the floor and let a strap do the reaching.
    • Full guide
    • Intermediate
  • Cat Pose
    Mārjaryāsana
    On hands and knees, rounding the spine toward the ceiling on an exhale. Nearly always paired with Cow.
    • Beginner
    • No prop
  • Cow Pose
    Bitilāsana
    On hands and knees, letting the belly descend and the chest open on an inhale. The counterpart to Cat.
    • Beginner
    • No prop
  • Standing Forward Fold
    Uttānāsana
    Folding forward from standing with the knees generously soft. Blocks under the hands turn it from a reach into a rest.
    • Beginner
    • Blocks help
  • Low Lunge
    Añjaneyāsana
    Front foot planted, back knee down, hips settling forward. Fold a blanket under the back knee and it changes entirely.
    • Beginner
    • Blanket helps
  • High Lunge
    Aśva Sañcalānāsana
    A lunge with the back knee lifted and the back heel high. Blocks under the hands take the balance question out of it.
    • Intermediate
    • Blocks help
  • Triangle Pose
    Utthita Trikoṇāsana
    A wide stance with a straight front leg and the torso tipped sideways over it. The lower hand belongs on a block, not the floor.
    • Intermediate
    • Block
  • Extended Side Angle
    Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana
    From a Warrior II stance, the front forearm rests on the thigh or the hand reaches to a block, and the top arm reaches long overhead.
    • Intermediate
    • Block helps
  • Chair Pose
    Utkaṭāsana
    Knees bent, hips back, arms reaching forward or overhead, as though sitting down onto a seat that is not there.
    • Intermediate
    • No prop
  • Cobra Pose
    Bhujaṅgāsana
    Lying face down and lifting the chest with the hands lightly loaded. Height is not the goal; length along the front of the body is.
    • Beginner
    • No prop
  • Upward-Facing Dog
    Ūrdhva Mukha Śvānāsana
    Chest lifted with the arms straight and the thighs off the floor. Only the hands and the tops of the feet stay down.
    • Intermediate
    • No prop
  • Sphinx Pose
    Sālamba Bhujaṅgāsana
    Propped on the forearms while lying face down. A long, quiet shape that is often held for minutes rather than breaths.
    • Beginner
    • Bolster helps
  • Locust Pose
    Śalabhāsana
    Face down, lifting the chest, arms and legs away from the floor with no hand support. Muscular work along the whole back.
    • Intermediate
    • Blanket helps
  • Plank Pose
    Phalakāsana
    A straight line from heels to head with the weight in the hands and toes. Knees down is a complete version, not a lesser one.
    • Intermediate
    • No prop
  • Side Plank
    Vasiṣṭhāsana
    Balanced on one hand and the outer edge of one foot. Dropping the lower knee to the mat makes it available to most people.
    • Advanced
    • Block helps
  • Half Moon
    Ardha Candrāsana
    Balanced on one leg and one hand with the other leg lifted parallel to the floor. Practiced with the back against a wall until it is steady.
    • Advanced
    • Block and wall
  • Pigeon Pose, preparation
    Eka Pāda Rājakapotāsana
    Front shin angled across the mat with the back leg long behind. Needs a bolster or folded blanket under the front hip to sit level.
    • Intermediate
    • Bolster
  • Reclined Figure Four
    Supta Kapotāsana
    On the back with one ankle crossed over the opposite thigh. The floor does the work that balance would otherwise have to.
    • Beginner
    • Strap helps
  • Seated Twist
    Ardha Matsyendrāsana
    Seated with one knee crossed over and the torso turning toward it. Sit on a folded blanket so the twist starts from a level pelvis.
    • Intermediate
    • Blanket
  • Reclined Twist
    Supta Matsyendrāsana
    On the back with both knees dropping to one side. A bolster between or under the knees keeps the shoulders on the floor.
    • Beginner
    • Bolster helps
  • Bound Angle
    Baddha Koṇāsana
    Seated with the soles of the feet together and the knees falling wide. Blocks under both knees turn it into a restorative shape.
    • Beginner
    • Blocks help
  • Wide-Legged Forward Fold
    Prasārita Pādottānāsana
    Feet wide and parallel, folding forward between them. Blocks or a chair seat catch the hands long before the floor does.
    • Intermediate
    • Blocks
  • Head-to-Knee Forward Bend
    Jānu Śīrṣāsana
    One leg long, the other foot drawn to the inner thigh, folding over the straight leg. A strap around the foot keeps the spine long.
    • Intermediate
    • Strap
  • Happy Baby
    Ānanda Bālāsana
    On the back holding the outer edges of both feet with the knees drawn toward the armpits. Straps replace hands when the reach is long.
    • Beginner
    • Strap helps
  • Legs Up the Wall
    Viparīta Karaṇī
    Lying on the back with the legs resting vertically against a wall. Frequently held for five to fifteen minutes.
    • Beginner
    • Wall
  • Corpse Pose
    Śavāsana
    Lying flat on the back, arms a little away from the sides, doing nothing on purpose. Ends nearly every practice.
    • Beginner
    • Blanket helps
  • Easy Seat
    Sukhāsana
    A simple cross-legged seat. Comfortable only when the hips sit higher than the knees, which usually means sitting on something.
    • Beginner
    • Blanket
  • Hero Pose
    Vīrāsana
    Kneeling with the shins beside the hips and sitting between the heels, usually on a block. Asks a lot of the knees and ankles.
    • Intermediate
    • Block
  • Camel Pose
    Uṣṭrāsana
    Kneeling upright and leaning back with the hands on the heels or on blocks beside the ankles. A large backbend that rewards patience.
    • Advanced
    • Blocks
  • Half Dog at the Wall
    Ardha Adho Mukha Śvānāsana
    Hands on a wall at hip height, feet walked back until the torso is horizontal. The same shoulder work as Downward Dog with none of the wrist load.
    • Beginner
    • Wall

If you are starting today

Five poses worth learning first

Thirty-eight entries is a lot to meet at once. These five come up in nearly every beginner class, they cover standing, resting, folding and lifting between them, and each one has a chair or wall version written out. Learn these and most of a class will already be familiar.

  1. Child's Pose, because rest is a skill

    Learn it first, not last. One to three minutes with an eight-count exhale, forehead on a block. It is the shape you will fall back on in every other practice, and it only works if you have set it up before you need it. Read the full guide.

  2. Warrior II, because standing work has to start somewhere

    Nothing complicated to remember, and five to eight breaths a side will tell you exactly where your legs currently are. The seated chair version is the same pose in office clothes. Read the full guide.

  3. Tree Pose, because balance is trainable

    Thirty seconds a side beside a wall, most days. It fits in the gap while a kettle boils, and two weeks of it changes how much correcting your ankle has to do. Read the full guide.

  4. Bridge Pose, because it runs two ways

    Five to eight breaths of real leg work, or two to five minutes resting on a block doing nothing at all. Knowing both gives you something for a morning and something for a Tuesday night. Read the full guide.

  5. Downward-Facing Dog, once your wrists are ready

    Deliberately last. Start at a wall for a fortnight, then on blocks, and only then on the floor with the knees bent. Rushing this one is the most common way beginners end up with sore wrists. Read the full guide.

  • Order Rest first, standing second, weight-bearing last.
  • Time All five fit inside fifteen minutes once you know them.
  • Kit A blanket, a block and a wall cover every version above.

Reading the tags

How to read a Stillform pose card

A prop tag names what opens the pose up, not a shopping list. Triangle Pose carries a block tag because a block is what lets most people keep the spine long instead of collapsing sideways to reach the floor. Practising it without one is still practising it.

Difficulty describes the coordination a pose asks for, and nothing else. It is not a ranking of the people doing it. Plenty of practitioners ten years in spend most of their week inside poses tagged beginner, because that is where the useful work lives.

Six entries open into a full guide. Those six were picked because they turn up in nearly every beginner class, and because each one is routinely taught with something important left out. The rest are being written.

A prop tag is not a barrier to entry. It is the thing that removes one.
