Equipment

A block is equipment, not an apology

Taking the block feels like admitting something. It is not. A prop does not reduce a pose, it removes the reason the pose was deforming, and the practitioners with the longest histories are the ones who own the most of them.

Published 05/13/2026 Revised 08/27/2026 5 minutes By Stillform editorial

The argument

There is a moment in most beginner classes when the teacher offers blocks and part of the room declines. Not after weighing it up and deciding the block would not help. Because reaching for one feels like conceding something in front of strangers.

That instinct is worth pulling apart, because it rests on a mistaken idea of what the object on the floor is for.

A block does not make a pose easier. It makes it a different pose.

Take Triangle. Feet wide, front leg straight, torso tipping sideways over it, lower hand travelling toward the floor.

For most people the floor is not reachable with a long spine. Something has to give, and what gives is the spine. It bends sideways, the chest rotates down toward the mat, and the hand lands somewhere on the shin. From across the room the pose now looks about right.

What is actually happening is that a shape meant to lengthen one side of the torso while the legs work has turned into a shape where the torso folds and the hamstrings complain. Those are not two versions of one pose. They are two different activities that happen to share an outline.

Put a block under the lower hand and the geometry changes. The floor has arrived early. The spine stays long, the chest stays open to the side of the room, and the legs go back to doing the work the pose was built around. That is the pose. Without the block you were doing something else.

A prop does not reduce a pose. It removes the reason the pose was deforming.

The one sentence this article exists for

Three things a prop does, and there is no fourth

Every use of every prop is one of three mechanisms.

  • It brings the floor closer. A block under a hand, a hip or a forehead. The ground arrives at a height you can meet without distorting to get there.
  • It makes your arms longer. A strap around the feet in a seated fold. Your reach is now long enough that your spine does not have to round to make up the difference.
  • It carries your weight. A bolster under the torso in Child's Pose, a block under the pelvis in Bridge. Nothing has to hold you up, which is the only reason a five-minute hold is possible.

None of those is the same as making a pose less demanding. The third one creates a pose that does not otherwise exist. A supported Bridge held for four minutes is not an easy version of active Bridge. It is a thing that only happens with a block.

The people who use the most props have practised the longest

Walk into an Iyengar room, the lineage most associated with prop use, and you will find blocks, straps, bolsters, blankets, chairs and ropes bolted to the wall. The teacher, who may be thirty years and a long formal training into this, will be using them alongside everyone else.

Most of the prop vocabulary the rest of yoga borrows came out of that tradition, and it did not arrive as a kindness to beginners. It arrived as a way of making a specific action available to a specific body, so that experienced people could work on what the pose was for instead of on what their proportions made awkward.

Restorative practice makes the point more bluntly. A restorative class is not a beginner class. It is a format built entirely from props, where nothing happens without them, practised by people at every level.

Reach is mostly skeleton

This is the part worth sitting with for a minute.

How far you fold forward in a seated forward fold is set by several things you did not choose and cannot train: the length of your femurs against your torso, the depth and angle of your hip sockets, the shape of the top of your thigh bone, and how far your pelvis tips before your spine has to take over.

Two people with identical hamstring length can sit eight inches apart in the same pose, permanently, because the gap is skeletal.

Why reaching your feet measures nothing

So reaching your feet is not a measure of your practice. It is a measure of your proportions. A strap is not a stand-in for the real version. It is what lets a long-femured person perform the same hip hinge that a short-femured person gets without one.

The same holds for heels in Downward-Facing Dog, palms flat in a standing fold, and whether Triangle needs a block. All three are widely treated as milestones. None of them is one.

How this site handles props

Every pose guide here carries at least two prop-supported options, written as equal alternatives rather than as fallbacks. The wall version of Downward-Facing Dog is not the beginner version. It is the version that gives you the shoulder action without the wrist load, and for a great many people it is the right choice permanently.

The props guide lists a household substitute for every item on it, because nothing here requires a purchase. A hardback book is a block. A bathrobe belt is a strap. A folded bath towel is a blanket. Two firm pillows in one pillowcase is a bolster.

And a prop tag in the pose library names the prop that opens a pose up. It is not a list of things you must own. Triangle carries a block tag because a block is what keeps most spines long in it. Practising Triangle without one is still practising Triangle.

What to do about it on Thursday

Take the block. Not because you need it and not because you do not. Take it, use it in one pose where you would normally reach without it, and pay attention to what changes.

Most people find that what changes is not how hard the pose is but where they feel it. That is the entire argument, and it is far more convincing in a body than on a page.

Four lines

What to take away

The whole piece compressed, for anyone standing at the prop shelf deciding whether to reach for something.

  • Different, not easier A block changes the geometry of a pose rather than lowering its demand.
  • Three mechanisms Props bring the floor closer, lengthen your reach, or hold your weight.
  • Bones decide reach Femur length and hip sockets set how far you fold, and practice does not change them.
  • Nothing to buy A hardback book, a bathrobe belt and a folded towel cover most of it.
