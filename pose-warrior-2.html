Warrior II

Vīrabhadrāsana II  (veer-ah-bah-DRAH-suh-nuh)

Feet wide, front knee bent, arms reaching in opposite directions at shoulder height. The name comes from Vīrabhadra, a figure out of an old story. For most people this is the first pose that makes it obvious what standing work actually costs the legs, and the first one where forty-five seconds starts to feel like a decision.

Difficulty
Beginner. Nothing here is complicated. The cost is endurance, not coordination.
Breath pattern
Even. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts, through the nose, right to the last second.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths a side, which works out around 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
External rotation at the front hip, quadriceps and glutes under load, ankle stability on both sides, plain endurance in the shoulders
Props that help
A sturdy chair, a wall behind you, a block under the front thigh
Where it appears
Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Stand tall, feet together, arms hanging at your sides.
  • Step the feet wide apart. Three to four feet is typical, and taller bodies need more. A quick check: with the arms out, your ankles should sit roughly under your wrists.
  • Turn the right foot out ninety degrees so the toes aim at the short edge of the mat.
  • Angle the left foot in about fifteen degrees. That back foot stays fully weighted, and the outer edge in particular stays down.
  • Line your right heel up with the arch of your left foot.
  • Bend the right knee toward a right angle, keeping the kneecap tracking out over the second and third toes.
  • Reach the arms out to shoulder height, palms facing the floor, and let the shoulders drop away from your ears.
  • Turn your head to look out past your right hand, keeping the back of the neck long.
Alignment cues
  • Knee over ankle, never past it. If the knee has travelled beyond the ankle, the answer is a wider stance, not a shallower bend.
  • Send the front knee outward. It will want to drift in toward your big toe. Press the outer edge of the front foot down and think of guiding the knee gently toward the little-toe side.
  • Stack the ribs over the pelvis. Your torso stays upright and centred between the two legs. Drifting toward the front thigh is the most common slide, and it happens around breath four.
  • The back leg is a working leg. Straight, switched on, outer heel pressing down. It is not a kickstand propped behind you.
  • Arms level, not lifted. Reach out through both sets of fingertips in opposite directions rather than hoisting the shoulders up toward the ears.
  • Hips face the long edge of the mat. Both hip points aim out to the side, not forward over the bent knee.
Common mistakes
  • A stance that is too short. The usual reason a front knee ends up past the ankle. Step wider before you bend deeper. It is nearly always the fix.
  • The back heel peeling up. The back foot loses the floor and the whole structure floats. Shorten the stance until that outer heel stays reliably down.
  • Leaning out over the front thigh. It feels like effort and is actually a collapse. Bring the ribs back over the pelvis and you will feel the legs take the work again.
  • Shoulders climbing toward the ears. Reach wide rather than up. If the arms will not stay level for the whole hold, lower them and keep the legs.
  • Front knee falling inward. This is about strength and attention, not flexibility. Press the outer edge of the front foot down and take the bend a little shallower.
  • Losing the breath in the last few seconds. Reliable evidence that the hold is currently too long. Cut it to three or four breaths and build from there.
Accessible modifications

All four are the pose. Pick whichever one lets you keep an even breath from the first count to the last.

Seated in a chair

Sit sideways on a sturdy chair with your right thigh along the seat and the right knee bent over the front edge. Stretch the left leg out to the side, foot turned slightly in, and open the arms wide. Same rotation at the hip, same work in the arms, same breath, with the chair carrying your bodyweight instead of your legs. It is the version used in the Desk Reset sequence, and it works in ordinary clothes.

Back against a wall

Stand with your back and both heels lightly against a wall, then build the pose from there. The wall reports on you: the moment your torso drifts forward or your hips start to turn, you feel the contact change. It also removes the balance question, which frees your attention for the legs.

Shallower knee bend

Take the front knee to forty-five degrees rather than ninety. This is a real version and a permanent one, not a rung on a ladder. If your knees are sensitive, or standing work is new to you, start here and stay for as long as it keeps being useful.

Arms lowered

Put your hands on your hips, or let the arms hang. Holding the arms out at shoulder height is its own separate piece of endurance work, and there is no good reason to let your shoulders be the thing that ends a leg pose early.

Progression

Once eight breaths a side feel steady with the front knee at ninety degrees and the breath quiet throughout, add breaths rather than depth: work toward twelve. After that this stance becomes the doorway to two other poses. Extended Side Angle rests the front forearm on the thigh and reaches the top arm long overhead. Triangle Pose straightens the front leg and tips the torso sideways over it with the lower hand on a block.

Contraindications and cautions

Take care, take the chair version, or take a shallower knee bend if you have knee pain, a recent knee or hip injury or replacement, an unstable ankle, sacroiliac discomfort, or a shoulder injury that makes holding the arms out painful. The same goes in later pregnancy, when a wide stance can feel unsteady. Come out if you feel pinching at the front of the hip, sharp sensation on the inside of the knee, or if your breath simply will not stay even.

The chair version

The chair version, step by step

This is the full pose with a chair under you instead of your own legs. It works in office clothes, it works with sore knees, and it works on a day when standing for forty-five seconds is not the thing you want to spend your energy on. Use a chair with four legs on the floor, not a wheeled office chair.

  1. Sit sideways along the seat

    Turn to sit on the chair sideways, with your right thigh running along the seat and your right knee bent over the front edge. Your right shin should drop straight down, ankle under knee, exactly as it would standing.

  2. Stretch the left leg out to the side

    Extend the left leg away from you along the line of the chair, straight and switched on, with the foot turned slightly in and the outer edge pressing the floor. This is your back leg and it has the same job it has on the mat.

  3. Sit up out of the pelvis

    Lengthen from the sitting bones to the crown of your head and bring your ribs back over your pelvis. Both hip points face out to the side, the same direction they would face standing.

  4. Open the arms to shoulder height

    Reach both arms out, palms down, shoulders dropping away from your ears. Hands on the hips is a complete option if the shoulders are the part that tires first.

  5. Turn the head, then hold

    Look out past the right hand with the back of your neck long. Stay five to eight even breaths, then swap the chair around and build the whole thing again facing the other way.

  • Breath Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4, through the nose.
  • Hold Five to eight breaths a side, standing or seated.
  • Prop A chair with four legs on the floor. Nothing on castors.

The leg work

Difficult without being complicated

This is usually the pose where a beginner finds out that hard and complicated are separate things. There is nothing to work out here. You can read the shape from across a room. Then forty-five seconds pass, the front thigh is burning, the arms want to come down, and it turns out that was the whole exercise.

Which is exactly why the forty-five degree knee bend deserves more respect than it gets. A shallow bend held with an even breath and the ribs stacked over the pelvis teaches your legs more than a deep bend held with your torso collapsed forward and your breath stopped. The second version looks better in a photograph. The first one is the one doing something.

The other thing worth knowing is how much of this pose lives in your back foot. Let that outer heel lift even slightly and the base goes, at which point the front knee quietly takes on more than its share. Shortening your stance until the heel stays down is one of the highest-value adjustments available anywhere in standing work, and it costs nothing.

Step wider before you bend deeper. Nearly every problem in this pose answers to that one sentence.
