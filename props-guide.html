Equipment

Props, and what each one is actually for

What each prop is actually for, how to size it, what to buy and in which order, which things already in your house do the same job, and how to rebuild common poses around a chair, a wall, a block, a strap or a blanket.

Every prop does one of three jobs. It brings the floor up to meet you, it makes your arms longer, or it carries the weight of some part of you so that no muscle has to. That is the entire theory, and everything below is one of those three applied somewhere specific.

Here is the part worth holding onto: a prop changes what a pose does. It does not make a pose easier. Put a block under your hand in Triangle Pose and your spine can stay long, which turns the shape into a side-body pose rather than a negotiation with your hamstrings. Different pose, not a reduced one. The longer argument is here.

A block does not make a pose easier. It makes it a different pose, and usually a better-taught one.

The mat

What it does
Gives grip, a defined working area and consistent padding under the knees
Standard size
68 by 24 inches. Long sizes run 72 to 84 inches
Thickness
3 to 5 mm for standing work. 6 mm or more for joint comfort
Household substitute
A folded blanket over a rug, or a bare low-pile carpet

Taller than about 5 feet 10 inches? Buy the 72-inch or longer version so your head and your heels are both on the mat when you lie down. It is the sizing decision people most often wish they had made differently.

Thickness is a real trade-off rather than a quality ladder. A thin mat gives you a stable base under a standing pose and lets you feel the floor through your feet. A thick mat is kinder to knees and hip bones but makes balance work noticeably harder, because the surface shifts under your foot. Plenty of people who practice often keep a thin mat and slide a folded blanket under their knees when the floor gets unfriendly.

Grip beats material marketing. If your hands creep forward in Downward-Facing Dog, that is a grip problem, and no amount of technique will solve it.

Three heights

Blocks

What it does
Brings the floor up to your hand, hip or head
Standard size
9 by 6 by 4 inches, giving three usable heights
How many
Two. Having two matters more than what they cost
Household substitute
A thick hardback book, a shoebox packed tight, a stack of magazines taped together

Cork blocks are heavier and denser and they stay where you put them. Foam blocks are lighter, quieter when dropped, and a little gentler under a bony hip. Both work. Take cork if you will stand on a block or put real weight through it, and foam for supported and restorative work.

The three heights are the whole point of the shape. Flat on the floor a block is 4 inches, on its side 6 inches, standing on end 9 inches. The same pose will want different heights on different days, and often a different height on each side of your body.

Where a block goes:

  • Under the lower hand in Triangle Pose or Half Moon, so the spine stays long.
  • Under both hands in Standing Forward Fold or Low Lunge, so your arms reach the ground.
  • Under the forehead in Child's Pose, so the head rests rather than hangs.
  • Under the pelvis in supported Bridge Pose, so the hips can stay there for several minutes.
  • Between the thighs in Bridge Pose, so the knees hold hip-width without you managing it.
  • Under the sitting bones in Hero Pose, so the ankles and knees are not asked to fold the whole way.

Straps

What it does
Makes your arms longer so your spine does not have to round to reach
Standard length
6 feet suits most people. 8 or 10 feet for taller bodies or behind-the-back work
Buckle
D-ring or cinch. Both work. D-ring holds tension better
Household substitute
A bathrobe belt, a long scarf, a luggage strap, a dog leash

The strap is the most neglected prop in home practice and the one that rewrites the most poses. Any time you are reaching toward a foot and your back is rounding to get there, a strap ends the problem on the spot.

Where a strap goes:

  • Around the balls of both feet in Seated Forward Fold, held with the elbows soft.
  • Around one foot in Head-to-Knee Forward Bend or any reclined leg stretch.
  • Around the back thigh in reclined figure four, so your hands are not gripping for two minutes.
  • Looped around both thighs above the knees in Bridge Pose, giving the legs something to press out against.
  • Held between both hands for shoulder work, at whatever width lets the arms stay straight.

Blankets

What it does
Raises the hips, pads a joint, supports the head, keeps you warm
What to look for
Firm and densely woven. A fluffy blanket compresses to nothing
How many
Two is a good number for home practice
Household substitute
A folded bath towel, a firm woven throw, a folded flat bedsheet

The Mexican-style studio blanket exists for one reason: folded, it is firm enough to keep its shape under a body. Anything with that density does the same job. A duvet or a fleece throw does not, because it flattens the moment you sit on it and leaves you back on the floor.

The most useful application is also the least interesting to look at. Sit on the front edge of a folded blanket in any seated pose, so your hips end up higher than your knees. For a large share of adults that single change is the difference between a forward fold that happens at the hips and one that happens in the lower back.

Other places it earns its keep: rolled under the ankles in Child's Pose and Hero Pose, rolled behind the knees to cut down how far the knee has to bend, under the shoulders in Bridge Pose to protect the curve of the neck, under the knees in final rest, and spread over you during a long rest when your temperature drops.

Bolsters

What it does
Holds the weight of the torso so no muscle has to work
Shapes
Round is firmer and higher. Rectangular is flatter and more stable
Typical size
About 25 inches long, 6 to 9 inches deep
Household substitute
Two firm bed pillows inside one pillowcase, or a tightly rolled duvet

A bolster is what turns a long hold into something you can actually stay in. Across restorative and Yin work, the gap between three comfortable minutes and ninety miserable seconds is almost always a bolster in the right place.

Rectangular is the better first purchase. It is stable, it stacks with folded blankets, and it lies flat under a torso. Round bolsters give you more height and suit work that opens across the chest.

Two firm pillows inside one pillowcase genuinely does the job. The pillowcase is not optional: it is what stops the pillows sliding apart the moment you lie down on them.

Costs nothing

The two free props

The wall

A clear stretch of wall is the most useful prop in most homes, and nobody has to buy it. It gives feedback that no spoken cue can match. Stand with your back to a wall in Warrior II and you know instantly whether your torso is drifting forward. Put two fingertips on it in Tree Pose and the fear of falling disappears, which is frequently the thing that was making balance hard in the first place. Set your hands on it at hip height in Downward-Facing Dog and you get the whole shoulder action with no load through the wrists.

Three feet of clear wall covers most uses, with about four feet of floor in front of it. Check for baseboards and radiators before you commit to a spot.

The chair

A sturdy chair with no wheels turns almost any pose into a version you can do today. It holds your hands in a forward fold, takes a lifted foot in a lunge, becomes the seat for a seated Warrior II, and carries your calves in place of a wall for legs-up.

What it needs: a flat seat, no castors, no swivel, and no armrests if you want to fold forward between your knees. A dining chair is the ideal. A folding chair works as long as it locks properly.

The whole Desk Reset sequence is built on a chair, and every pose guide on this site writes a chair or wall option in as an equal alternative rather than a footnote.

Spending, in order

What to buy first, second and never

Nothing on this site requires a purchase. If you do want to buy, the order below spreads the cost across months and puts the money where it changes the most poses. Prices are rough US retail ranges and move around.

First: a mat, then two blocks

A mat is the one thing with no good free substitute if your floor is slippery, and the right length matters more than the brand. Two blocks come next, because a single block strands you halfway through half the poses that need one. Expect roughly $30 to $80 for a mat and $15 to $30 for a pair of blocks.

Second: a strap, a blanket, a bolster

Add a 6-foot strap once you meet a seated forward fold, a firm blanket once you start sitting on the floor for more than a minute, and a rectangular bolster only once you are regularly doing long supported holds. Roughly $10 to $15, $25 to $45 and $50 to $90 in that order.

Never, or not yet

Skip the wheel, the inversion stool, the branded towel set, the second mat for travel and anything sold as a bundle for beginners. None of them appear anywhere on this site, and each one is a purchase made instead of a practice.

  • Two beats one Two cheap blocks are worth more than one expensive block, every time.
  • Buy after the pose, not before Let a prop earn its place by appearing in something you already practice.
  • Borrow first Studios lend props during class. Use theirs for a month and you will know what you actually reach for.

Already in the house

Household substitutes that actually work

Not every improvised prop is a good one. These are the swaps that hold up under real use, and the few that people try first and then abandon.

Household objects that substitute for yoga props, how to prepare each one, and substitutes that do not work
Instead ofUseHow to make it work
BlockA stack of thick hardback booksStack two or three and put the largest on the bottom. A rubber band around the stack stops the top one sliding. Do not stand on it.
BlockA shoebox packed tightFill it with folded clothes until the lid does not flex under your hand. Roughly block height already.
StrapA bathrobe belt or a long scarfAnything about 6 feet long that does not stretch. Tie a loose knot at one end for a foot loop.
StrapA luggage strap or a dog leashBoth hold tension well. Pad the buckle with a sock if it sits under a foot.
BolsterA folded blanket, or two firm pillows in one pillowcaseFold the blanket in thirds and then in half for height. The single pillowcase is what stops two pillows sliding apart.
BolsterA tightly rolled duvetRoll it as hard as you can and tie it in two places with a belt or scarf. Re-roll it when it sags.
BlanketA folded bath towelFold to four layers. Dense enough to lift your hips and to pad a knee.
MatA folded blanket over low-pile carpetFine for floor work. Not for standing poses, where you need grip and a firm base.
Eye coverA folded washclothLay it across the eyes with no pressure on the eyeballs.
WallThe side of a sturdy bookcase or a closed doorA door must be shut and latched. Never use one that opens away from you.

Three substitutes worth skipping

  • Sofa cushions as a bolster. Too soft and too wide. They compress to nothing under a torso and then slide out sideways.
  • A rolled yoga mat under the hips. It unrolls itself the moment you settle onto it, usually a minute into a five-minute hold.
  • A swivel or wheeled office chair. The one chair to avoid entirely. If it turns or rolls, it is not a prop.

You can start with none of it

A sturdy chair, a clear wall, a folded bath towel, a hardback book and a bathrobe belt between them cover every modification described on this page. That is not a compromise version of the practice. It is the practice.

If and when you do buy, the order is mat, two blocks, strap, blanket, bolster, and it reflects how many poses each one changes rather than what each one costs.

Health and safety

Props change how a pose loads your body. They do not make any pose risk-free, and nothing on this page treats or prevents any health condition. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before starting a new physical practice, particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition. Stop if something hurts, and work with a qualified teacher for hands-on guidance. Full disclaimer.
