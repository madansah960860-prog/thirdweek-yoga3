Teacher directory

Find a teacher near you

Yoga teachers and studios across the United States. A basic listing is free and stays free. Featured placement is paid and is labeled as featured wherever it shows up. What we check and what we do not is set out below, in full, before you use any of it.

Read this before you use the directory

Stillform does not vet, endorse, certify, employ or supervise anyone listed here. Teachers and studios write their own entries and we publish them largely as submitted. Check credentials, insurance and experience yourself before you book. The rest of this page is about how to do that in a way that takes one class rather than one month.

Two tiers

How the directory works

There are two ways to appear here. The difference between them is where you sit on the page, and nothing else.

  • Basic listing. Free, and it stays free. Business name, city and state, formats taught, whether the space has step-free access, a contact link and a website. Sorted alphabetically inside each state. Nobody pays to be here.
  • Featured placement. Paid. Sits above the basic listings for its metro area, with a photograph and a longer description. Every one carries a visible Featured placement label wherever it appears. It buys position and room to write. It does not buy an endorsement, a review or any claim about how good the teaching is.

Inside each tier the order is alphabetical. No ranking, no stars, no score out of five. We have no way to measure teaching quality from Portland, and inventing a number would be worse than admitting that.

We can tell you a studio exists and what it says about itself. We cannot tell you it is good.

01 / In the room

One class is enough

How to vet a teacher in one class

You do not need six weeks to work out whether a teacher suits you. One class tells you most of it, if you know where to look. Here is the hour, in order.

  1. Before it starts: did anyone ask?

    A teacher who greets a new face and asks about injuries, recent surgery, pregnancy or anything they should know is a teacher who intends to use the answer. One who does not ask will be teaching the room rather than the people in it. Arrive ten minutes early and see which one you get.

  2. First ten minutes: are the props already out?

    Blocks and blankets stacked at the front, offered by name, used by the teacher in their own demonstration: that is a room where props are equipment. Props kept in a cupboard and fetched only when someone struggles is a room where they read as a failure. The first framing is the one you want.

  3. Mid-class: count the options

    Listen for two or three versions of the same pose, given in the same tone, without one being labeled the real one. Listen for a teacher naming what a pose is for, not only what it looks like. And listen for permission to stop, offered once, early, without drama attached to it.

  4. Hands-on: were you asked first?

    Consent should be sought before anyone touches you, through a card, a raised hand at the start, or a question in the moment. A teacher who adjusts without asking has told you something about the rest of their practice. Declining is a complete sentence and needs no reason after it.

  5. Last five minutes: is there an ending?

    A class that finishes on a rushed final pose and a fast goodbye is a class that ran out of plan. Time set aside to lie down, and a minute before you stand up, is a sign of someone who sequences on purpose.

  6. Afterward: how do you feel tomorrow?

    Pleasantly worked is normal. Sharp joint pain the next morning is not, and it is worth telling the teacher about. How they respond to that message is the last piece of information you need.

  • Walk out Leaving mid-class is allowed anywhere, for any reason, and you owe no one an explanation.
  • Rest early Taking a break before you need one is a skill, not a retreat.
  • Ask twice If a cue made no sense, ask after class. A good teacher says it a second way.
02 / Before you pay

Say these out loud

Questions worth asking before you pay

Ask these before a class pass or a package leaves your account. None of them are rude, and every working teacher has answered all of them before.

  • What training did you complete, with whom, and over what period? The signal is a specific answer. Vagueness about where and when is the thing to notice.
  • Do you carry professional liability insurance? Most working teachers in the United States do, and the answer should come back immediately.
  • How long have you taught this particular format? Someone can be ten years into Vinyasa and three months into Restorative. Worth knowing which one you are booking.
  • How do you handle hands-on adjustments? A good answer describes a consent process, not a philosophy of touch.
  • What happens if I need to change something mid-class? The answer tells you whether props are standard equipment or an exception made for you.
  • Can I take a single class before buying a package? Almost everywhere says yes. A studio that only sells blocks of ten to a first-time visitor is telling you about its business, not its teaching.
  • What is the refund and expiry policy on a class pass? Ask in writing. Passes that expire in 30 days are a different product from passes that do not.
  • Is the schedule level label yours, or an industry standard? It is always theirs. Level one at one studio moves faster than level two at another, so read the class description rather than the label.

On credentials in general: registration with a teaching organization records that a training was finished. It does not assess an individual teacher's skill, and it is not a healthcare qualification. A yoga teacher is not a physical therapist, a physician or a clinician, and a good one will say so before you do.

If you need specific accommodations

Call or email ahead rather than asking at the door. Worth confirming: step-free entry and elevator access, whether chairs are kept in the room, whether music plays and how loudly, whether the lights come down, whether mats and props are provided, and how the changing rooms are arranged.

Studios that answer those questions quickly and in detail are the ones to go to. A vague answer to a specific access question is itself an answer.
