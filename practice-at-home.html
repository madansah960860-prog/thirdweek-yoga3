Home practice

Setting up a practice at home

The amount of floor you genuinely need, how to test the surface you have, what to do about carpet, thin ceilings and housemates, and how to build a routine that is still standing after a week that went badly.

Space and floor

How much space you need

Less than you are picturing, and the figure is specific. Lie on the floor and stretch both arms past your head. The rectangle you take up, plus about a foot in every direction, is the whole requirement. For most adults that comes to roughly 7 feet by 4 feet, or around 28 square feet.

Two more things are worth having, and neither costs anything:

  • Three feet of clear wall, with four feet of floor in front of it. Look for baseboards, radiators, light switches and picture hooks sitting at shoulder height.
  • Enough ceiling to reach both arms overhead standing up. Obvious right up until you set up under a sloped attic ceiling and find that Mountain Pose does not fit.

A dedicated room is not required, and for most people wanting one is a way of making the practice conditional on having one. A cleared patch of living room works. So does the strip at the foot of a bed, or a hallway with the runner rolled back.

Twenty-eight square feet and a clear wall. That is the entire equipment list for a home practice.

Stillform, on what a home space actually needs

Testing the floor you have

The floor matters more than the room does. Three checks before your first practice.

  • Is it level? Put the mat down and stand on one foot near each end. An uneven floor makes balance work confusing rather than hard, because you cannot tell what is you and what is the room.
  • Does the mat travel? On polished hardwood or tile a mat can slide during a lunge. Put a thin rug or a folded blanket underneath. On laminate, press your hands into the mat as if holding a plank and see whether it shifts.
  • Is it firm enough? Deep-pile carpet or a thick rug makes standing poses wobbly and balance work considerably harder. Firm under the mat beats soft every time.

If all you have is carpet

Low pile is fine and needs nothing at all. Medium pile works with a mat on top. Deep pile or a thick rug pad is the one genuinely awkward surface, and there are two straightforward answers: slide a hard board or a large cutting board under the mat where your standing foot lands, or move to the kitchen or bathroom floor and bring a folded blanket for your knees.

If there is no floor space at all

Practice from a chair. That is a real option and not a consolation prize. The Desk Reset sequence needs nothing but a chair and a desk, and every pose guide on this site carries a chair or wall version. A studio apartment with one dining chair in it already has everything required.

One afternoon, ten minutes

Setting up a corner in ten minutes

You can build a workable practice corner today, with a timer running. Six steps, ten minutes, nothing bought.

  1. Minutes 0 to 2: pick the corner and clear it

    Walk the rooms you already spend time in and find the 7-by-4 rectangle with a clear wall along one long side. Move whatever is standing in it out and put it somewhere it will stay. A corner you have to clear each time is a corner you will stop using.

  2. Minutes 2 to 4: test the floor and the wall

    Unroll the mat. Stand on one foot at each end. Press your hands into the mat and check whether it slides. Run a hand along the wall for a thermostat, a switch plate or a picture hook at shoulder height, and move the picture if there is one.

  3. Minutes 4 to 6: gather the five things

    Two blocks or two thick hardbacks, a strap or a bathrobe belt, one folded blanket or bath towel, a cushion, and the chair. Stack them at one corner of the mat, or in a basket against the wall. Getting up mid-hold to fetch a block is the most common reason a home practice ends early.

  4. Minutes 6 to 8: decide where the phone lives

    If you are running a timer from a sequence page, the phone goes at the top of the mat, face down until you need it, with do-not-disturb on. If you are working from a printed sheet, the phone goes in another room. Decide once so you are not deciding daily.

  5. Minutes 8 to 9: print one sequence and leave it out

    Every sequence page here prints as a clean handout of two or three letter sheets, with the header, navigation, timer controls, ad slot, trailing commentary and footer stripped away, at a type size you can read from standing. Print the one you expect to do most and leave it beside the mat.

  6. Minute 10: leave the mat unrolled

    Friction, not motivation, is what predicts whether a home practice survives. A mat in a closet adds a decision every single day. A mat already on the floor removes one. If it truly cannot stay out, keep it rolled somewhere you walk past rather than somewhere you have to go.

  • Same corner every time A fixed spot removes the daily question of where to practice.
  • Props at the mat edge Anything you have to stand up for is a reason to stop early.
  • One printed sheet Paper never buzzes, never dims and never asks for a passcode.

The real obstacle

How to practice when the house is full

Space is rarely what stops a home practice. Other people in the room is what stops it, and the fix is logistical rather than emotional.

Say what you are doing, out loud, once

Trying to be discreet about it draws more attention than the practice ever would. One sentence, said in advance, settles the whole thing: I am going to be on the floor in the corner for twenty minutes, you can walk through, I am not meditating and you are not interrupting. That sentence does more than a closed door.

Aim for the quietest hour, not the empty house

Waiting for an empty house means waiting. Pick the hour when the household is at its slowest instead: early morning before anyone is up, the stretch after a small child goes down, or the middle of the evening once dinner is cleared. Consistency beats privacy.

With small children in the room

Expect to be climbed on and plan for it rather than around it. Put a second mat or a folded blanket beside yours and call it their spot. Choose floor-based sequences over balance work, since an interruption in Tree Pose costs more than one in Child's Pose. Ten minutes of the morning sequence with a toddler underfoot is a real practice, not a failed one.

When you share the only room

Practice facing a wall rather than the room. It narrows what you can see, which quietly narrows what you feel watched by, and it puts you within reach of the most useful prop in the apartment. Headphones with nothing playing work surprisingly well as a signal that you are mid-something.

Have a three-minute fallback ready

Decide in advance what you do when the twenty minutes evaporates. Two minutes of Cat and Cow and one minute of Child's Pose is a complete practice on a day that came apart, and it keeps the run of days unbroken, which is the thing that actually matters.

Thin floors, small apartments and pets

If you live above other people, or below them

Almost none of this makes any noise. There are no jumps and no impact anywhere in the four sequences on this site. The loudest thing that happens is a mat unrolling. If you are still uneasy about it, the evening wind-down stays on the floor from start to finish.

Working with a small apartment

Use the wall you have rather than the room you would like. Practice along the longest clear line in the place, which is often the hallway. Roll the mat out perpendicular to the bed and let the bed frame stand in for a chair. A coffee table is frequently the exact height for a supported forward fold.

Pets

A cat will sit on the mat. This is universal and there is no fix. Put a folded blanket in one corner of the mat and the cat will usually take that instead. A dog reads Downward-Facing Dog as an invitation, which is useful to know before the first attempt rather than during it.

A routine that survives a bad week

Every home practice eventually runs into a week where it simply does not happen. Whether it continues afterward depends almost entirely on what you decided about that week before it arrived.

Three decisions that make the difference:

  • Anchor the practice to an event, not a clock. "After I put the coffee on" survives a changed schedule. "7:15 a.m." does not.
  • Set your minimum now. Three minutes counts. Naming the floor in advance means a bad day produces a short practice instead of no practice.
  • Resume rather than restart. Four missed days is four missed days, not a failure requiring a fresh beginning. The next practice is simply the next practice.

A weekly shape that works for a first few months, assuming nothing about your schedule beyond it having some gaps:

  • Three ten-minute mornings, whichever three come easiest.
  • One or two desk resets partway through a working day.
  • One evening wind-down.
  • One gentle full practice on your least rushed day, once that sounds appealing rather than daunting.

That comes to about ninety minutes a week, cut into pieces small enough to survive most weeks.
