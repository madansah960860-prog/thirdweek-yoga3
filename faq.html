First month

Getting started

No. Flexibility is a possible result of practicing, not a condition of starting. How far you travel into a shape is set largely by the proportions of your skeleton, which you did not choose. Two people with identical muscle length can sit eight inches apart in the same forward fold, permanently.

Every pose guide here carries at least two prop-supported setups, written as equal versions rather than as consolation prizes. Use the one that fits the range you have on the day you practice.

Nothing. A firm floor, clothes you can move in and a sturdy chair will carry you through the first month.

If you want one item after that, buy a mat, and buy a 72-inch one if you are taller than about 5 feet 10 inches. Then two blocks, then a strap, then a firm blanket. A hardback book works as a block, a bathrobe belt as a strap, a folded bath towel as a blanket. The props guide names a household substitute for every item on the list.

Ten to twenty minutes, three or four days a week. That is a workable first month for most adults. Frequency does more than duration at this stage, because what changes first is coordination, and coordination answers to repetition.

The beginner's guide sets out a four-week schedule built entirely from the free sequences.

Clothes you can kneel, bend and lie down in that will not slide over your head when you fold forward. Fitted at the ankle and the waist is more useful than fitted everywhere. Bare feet grip best; socks with rubber on the sole are the next option. Take off watches, belts and anything that will dig in when you lie on your back.

Yes, and it counts as a practice rather than a stand-in for one. The Desk Reset sequence is built around a sturdy chair, needs no mat and no change of clothes, and runs eight minutes.

Every pose guide on this site carries a chair or wall version, and every sequence names the chair alternative for each pose in its written beginner path.

Look for the words beginner, fundamentals or level one, in Hatha, Iyengar, Yin or Restorative. All four leave you time to hear an instruction and then do something with it. If Vinyasa is the only local option, take the slowest one on the schedule and skip anything labeled level two or power on a first visit. The styles page sets six formats side by side.

On the floor

Practicing on your own

Sensation is broad, sits over the belly of a muscle, and settles as you keep breathing. Effort is muscular work: the leg shakes, the breath wants to shorten. Pain is sharp, hot, electrical or pinching, usually sits in a joint rather than a muscle, and does not settle when you breathe.

Come out of anything that hurts. That is what people who have practiced for decades do, and it is a large part of why they are still practicing.

Because nearly every body is asymmetric. Handedness, how you sit at work, old injuries and occupation all leave a mark. Most people notice it in the second week, and noticing is a good sign: your attention has moved from making the shape to reading what is happening inside it.

Give both sides the same time, note the difference, carry on. Forcing the stiffer side to match the looser one is how people collect the injury they were trying to avoid.

No. The wobble is the ankle doing its job. It is the training rather than a sign of failing at the training, and it moves around day to day with tiredness, with the floor under you and with whatever you did yesterday.

Stand beside a wall and rest two fingertips on it. That removes the fear of falling, which is often the thing making balance hard in the first place. The Tree Pose guide works through it step by step.

Use the modification and call it the pose. Each guide lists at least two prop-supported setups, and each sequence names a specific alternative for every pose in it. Leaving a pose out altogether is also an ordinary thing to do and does not damage the sequence around it.

No. Age changes what a sensible practice looks like, not whether you get to have one. Balance work beside a wall, chair versions of standing poses, and longer holds with props underneath you are all full practice rather than a reduced version of it.

Get clearance from your doctor if you are managing anything, then begin with the Desk Reset sequence and the chair options in the pose library. Three sessions of ten minutes a week is a real beginning.

Yes, with setups that account for the body doing them. Most of what makes a pose awkward in a larger body is a question of space: where the belly goes in a forward fold, where the arms go in a twist, how the chest sits in a shape done face down.

The answers are practical. Widen the stance so the torso has somewhere to travel. Raise the floor with blocks instead of reaching further toward it. Take the twist from a chair, where the seat gives you leverage the floor does not. Every pose guide here lists the prop setups as options of equal standing, never as a lesser route.

Yes. Carpet handles everything except standing balance, where the pile makes the foot report the wrong information; do balance poses on a bare floor beside a wall instead. A bare floor handles everything except kneeling, which a folded towel under the knees solves in one move.

A bath towel doubled over gives you enough grip and padding to begin. Buy the mat once you already know you are practicing, rather than to persuade yourself that you will.

A rectangle the length of your body plus about a foot at each end, wide enough to put your arms straight out to the sides. Roughly seven feet by three. If you can lie down and sweep both arms without hitting furniture, you have enough.

Noise is rarely the problem people expect. Most of a beginner practice is slow, quiet and done on the floor. Step between poses rather than jumping, which is what every sequence on this site instructs anyway.
