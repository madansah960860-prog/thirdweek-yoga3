Bridge Pose

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana  (SAY-too BAHN-dah sar-vahn-GAH-suh-nuh)

You lie on your back, plant both feet, and lift your hips. Setu bandha means the forming of a bridge. It runs two completely different ways: as active work for the legs and hips, or with a block set under the pelvis so you can stay there, doing nothing, for several minutes.

Set-up, cues and every version

Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana

  • Beginner
  • Hips
  • Spine

Difficulty
Beginner either way, active or supported.
Breath pattern
Active: even, inhale 4 counts, exhale 4. Supported: inhale 4 counts, exhale 6.
Suggested hold
Active: 5 to 8 breaths. Supported on a block: 2 to 5 minutes, and it will feel longer.
What moves
Extension at the hip, hamstrings and glutes under load, extension through the spine, length across the front of the shoulders
Props that help
A block, a bolster, a folded blanket under the shoulders, a strap looped round the thighs
Where it appears
Evening Wind-Down, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Lie on your back with the knees bent and both feet flat on the floor.
  • Walk the feet back toward you until your fingertips can just brush your heels. That is the right distance for your proportions, whatever anyone else is doing.
  • Set the feet hip-width apart, outer edges parallel to the edges of the mat.
  • Lay your arms alongside your body, palms facing down.
  • Press evenly through both feet, from the base of the big toe out to the outer heel.
  • Lift the hips away from the floor, leading with the pubic bone rather than arching the lower back.
  • Roll your shoulders slightly underneath you and let the top of the chest lift toward your chin.
  • Keep the back of the neck long and your eyes straight up at the ceiling. Do not turn your head while the hips are lifted.
Alignment cues
  • The height comes from your feet. Press the floor down and the hips rise. Squeezing the lower back to gain an inch is the wrong trade.
  • Keep the knees hip-width. They will drift outward the moment you stop watching. A block held lightly between the thighs settles this in one move and asks nothing of your attention.
  • Knees track forward over the toes, not falling inward and not splaying out past the outer edge of the foot.
  • Chest toward chin, never chin toward chest. The opening belongs at the top of the chest. Your neck keeps its natural curve and stays uncompressed.
  • Both feet fully weighted. Gripping toes or lifting inner edges mean the feet are too close. Walk them a little further from your hips.
  • Head absolutely still. Turning your head while the hips are loaded is the one thing to avoid outright in this pose.
Common mistakes
  • Feet parked too far from the hips. You get a hamstring cramp waiting to happen, and the hips never really leave the floor. Fingertips to heels, every time.
  • Letting the knees splay. The lift stops coming from the legs and collapses back into the lumbar spine. Put a block between the thighs.
  • Chasing height. How high your hips go is set by the proportions of your hips and spine, and it is not what this pose is measuring. Even is worth more than high.
  • Loading the neck. Pressure in the throat or the back of the neck means come down. Fold a blanket under your shoulders so the neck keeps its curve, then try again.
  • Turning the head mid-pose. Common, and worth breaking as a habit deliberately. Lower down first, then move your head.
  • Never trying the supported version. It is not an easier copy of the active one. It does something else entirely, and it deserves its own minutes.
Accessible modifications

All four are the pose. The supported one especially is not a scaled-down variant: it has a different job, a different hold time and a different breath.

Supported on a block

Lift the hips, slide a block under the bony part of your pelvis on its lowest or middle height, then let your weight down onto it. Nothing has to work now. Stay two to five minutes with a six-count exhale. If the block feels hard against the bone, swap it for a firm bolster or two folded blankets stacked.

Blanket under the shoulders

Fold a firm blanket into a neat rectangle and slide it under your shoulders so your head and neck rest lower than the tops of the shoulders. That keeps the natural curve at the back of the neck while the pose is loaded. This is standard set-up in a well-run class, not a special accommodation.

Strap around the thighs

Loop a strap around both thighs just above the knees and tighten it to hip-width. Now your legs have something to press outward against, and the knees track correctly on their own. It saves you thinking about the same cue for eight breaths straight.

Small lift only

Lift the hips an inch or two and stop there. For a great many people, particularly anyone with a sensitive lower back, that is the entire pose and always will be. A small even lift held with a steady breath beats a big lopsided one every time.

Progression

When eight breaths in the active version feel even throughout, add a set of slow lifts and lowers: inhale to rise, exhale to lower one vertebra at a time, six to eight rounds. Once that is comfortable, lift one foot a few inches off the floor while keeping both hips exactly level. It is considerably harder than it reads, and the hips will drop before you notice. Further along this line sits Wheel, a full backbend on hands and feet, and that one genuinely belongs in a room with a teacher in it.

Contraindications and cautions

Take care, or take the small-lift or supported version, if you have a neck injury or neck pain, a recent shoulder injury, a lower back injury, or uncontrolled high blood pressure. In the second and third trimesters of pregnancy, lying flat on the back for extended periods is generally not advised. Do not turn your head while the hips are lifted. Come out if you feel pressure in your head or throat, sharp sensation in the lower back, or cramping in the hamstrings.

Coming out

Getting out of the pose

Most pose instructions spend eight steps getting you in and half a sentence getting you out. In a backbend held on a block, the exit is where people actually get hurt, so here it is written properly. Both versions end the same way: slowly, and with your head still.

Out of the active version

Keep looking straight up. Exhale and lower your spine to the floor one vertebra at a time, starting at the top of the chest and finishing at the tailbone, so it takes a full breath rather than a sudden drop. Let the arms stay heavy. Once the hips are down, pause for two breaths before you move your head, then let the knees knock together and rest.

Off the block

Press both feet down and lift your hips just enough to unweight the block, an inch is plenty. Slide the block out to one side with one hand, then lower the spine down slowly, tailbone last. Never pull the block out while your pelvis is still resting on it. Stay flat for several breaths afterwards before you roll to one side and sit up.

  • Breath Active: inhale 4, exhale 4. Supported: inhale 4, exhale 6.
  • Hold Five to eight breaths active, two to five minutes on a block.
  • Prop A block, plus a folded blanket under the shoulders if your neck asks for it.

Come down first, then move your head. Never the other way round.

The exit rule for every supine backbend on this site

Two versions

Two poses sharing one name

Active Bridge and supported Bridge have a shape in common and nothing else. In the active version your legs are working, glutes and hamstrings are switched on, and thirty seconds is a genuine thirty seconds. In the supported version a block carries your pelvis, your legs do nothing whatsoever, and the useful duration is counted in minutes.

That is why sequences reach for them at different moments. The gentle full practice places the active version late in the standing work, while your legs are still awake and willing. The evening wind-down takes the supported version and holds it unhurried, because by then the point is stillness rather than effort.

If you can only fit one in, the supported version is the one almost everybody under-practises. Putting a block under your hips and doing nothing for four minutes is harder to justify to yourself than a set of repetitions. It is also, most weeks, the more useful of the two.
