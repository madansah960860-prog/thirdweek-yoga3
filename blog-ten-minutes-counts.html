Ninety minutes a week: a practice built for a full calendar

The standard advice assumes you have an hour. Most adults do not have one on a Tuesday. Here is a weekly shape made of four short sessions and one optional long one, plus the eleven minutes of overhead that sink most attempts before the first pose.

Published 06/24/2026 Revised 08/27/2026 5 minutes By Stillform editorial

Ninety minutes a week

Studio classes run sixty or seventy-five minutes, and that is before travel, changing, and the part where you stand in the lobby working out which mat is yours. Online classes are usually forty-five. Both are built around a block of time a great many adults simply do not have on a weekday.

So there is a whole category of person who wants to practise, tries to protect an hour, fails three weeks running, and concludes they are not the sort of person who does this. That conclusion is about a calendar. It is not about yoga.

What ten minutes can and cannot do

A ten-minute sequence can move the spine into flexion, extension, side bending and rotation, put weight through the hands and the feet, take the hips through their available range in a lunge, and finish with you standing still. That is a complete set of movements. It is not a compressed hour. It is fewer repetitions of the same things.

What ten minutes cannot do is take you far into anything. Long holds, restorative work, a five-minute final rest, an unhurried setup for something difficult: those need room, and they are worth having. They are additions to a practice, though, not the door into one.

The practical case is simpler than the physiological one. Four ten-minute practices happen. One forty-minute practice often does not.

Forty minutes taken in pieces across a week is more yoga than forty minutes scheduled once and missed.

The arithmetic this article runs on

In the first six months, frequency beats length

Most of what changes early is coordination rather than tissue. Knowing where your hips are without looking, finding a stance width without measuring, keeping the breath even while a leg is working: those are skills, and skills answer to repetition more than to session length.

Balance shows it most clearly. Thirty seconds per leg, most days for a month, produces a difference most people notice. Fifteen minutes of balance work once a fortnight does not, because the corrections your ankle makes get faster through frequent short exposure rather than through occasional long ones.

There is a memory argument too. Practise a sequence four times in a week and you know it. Practise it once a week and every session opens with trying to remember what comes next, which is attention spent on recall instead of on the pose.

A weekly shape that survives a full calendar

Roughly ninety minutes, cut into pieces small enough to defend. None of it needs a studio, a class time, or a change of clothes for the middle two.

  • Three mornings, ten minutes each. The 10-Minute Morning. Ten poses, ten minutes, a wall and a folded blanket.
  • One or two work breaks, eight minutes each. The Desk Reset, done from a chair in whatever you already have on, with nothing that reads as unusual from across an office.
  • One evening, twenty minutes. The Evening Wind-Down. All of it on the floor, all of it still, ten supported holds.
  • One long practice, forty minutes, when it fits. The Gentle Full Practice, on whichever day is least rushed. Genuinely optional.

A week that only allows the three mornings is a practice. A week that only allows two desk resets is also a practice.

The eleven minutes nobody counts

People who have tried and failed to build a practice usually did not fail at the practice. They failed at the overhead wrapped around it.

Three specific pieces of friction, and what removes each one.

  • Finding and unrolling the mat. Leave it out. If it cannot stay out, leave it rolled somewhere you already walk past rather than somewhere you have to go and fetch it. This one change does more than any amount of resolve.
  • Deciding what to do. Pick one sequence and repeat it. Choosing a practice each morning is a decision, and decisions at 6:40 a.m. lose to going back to bed. Print the sequence and leave the sheet next to the mat.
  • The phone. Using an on-screen timer, put the device face down at the top of the mat in do-not-disturb. Using paper, leave the phone in another room. A notification during the fourth pose is the single most common reason a ten-minute practice becomes a four-minute practice.

Attach it to something, not to a clock

A practice pinned to a time competes with everything else that could occupy that time. A practice pinned to something that already happens does not.

After I start the coffee survives a schedule change. After I close the laptop survives a meeting running long. Before I get in the shower survives almost anything. A 7:15 a.m. commitment survives none of them.

Find something that already happens every day without you deciding to do it, and hang the ten minutes off that.

Decide your three-minute version now

Every practice eventually meets a week that falls apart. What happens next depends almost entirely on whether the minimum was agreed in advance.

Three minutes: two minutes of Cat and Cow on hands and knees, one minute of Child's Pose. That is a legitimate practice. It moves the spine, it puts you on the floor, and it keeps the run of days unbroken.

The reason to settle it now is that on the day you need it you will be in no state to negotiate with yourself about what counts. Having already answered the question takes the negotiation off the table.

On the days you miss

Missing four days is four days. It is not a lapse calling for a fresh start, a new plan, or a conversation with yourself about commitment.

Streak language does real damage here, because it turns an ordinary gap into a failure that demands a response. There is no response. The next practice is simply the next practice, and it is worth exactly what it would have been worth if the four days had not happened.

Five lines

What to take away

The week in five sentences, for anyone who opened this page between two meetings.

  • Ninety minutes Three ten-minute mornings, one or two eight-minute breaks, one twenty-minute evening.
  • Often beats long In the first six months, repetition builds coordination faster than session length does.
  • Cut the overhead Leave the mat out, pick one sequence, put the phone face down.
  • Use an anchor Hang the practice off the coffee or the laptop lid, never off a clock time.
  • Three minutes counts Cat and Cow plus Child's Pose is a real practice on a bad day.
