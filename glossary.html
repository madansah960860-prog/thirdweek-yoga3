Vocabulary

Yoga vocabulary, defined

Sixty Sanskrit and English terms you will meet in a class or on this site, each with a plain pronunciation and a short definition. Diacritics are written throughout, because dropping them changes the word. There is also a note at the end on the phrases teachers use that do not mean anything precise.

Saying it out loud

A pronunciation primer

The guides in brackets throughout this page are approximations built from ordinary American English sounds. They are close enough to be understood in a studio, not linguistically exact. Five minutes with the rules below will get you further than memorizing sixty bracketed spellings.

Vowels carry the length

A line over a vowel is not decoration. It means hold that vowel roughly twice as long, and it is the single change that makes Sanskrit words sound right rather than flattened.

Three rules that cover most pose names

  • Stress falls on a long vowel when the word has one. In Āsana the stress lands at the front; in Tāḍāsana it lands on the second long "a".
  • The ending is always -āsana, said "AH-suh-nuh". Get that ending right and three quarters of every pose name is already correct.
  • Break the compound apart. Adho Mukha Śvānāsana is three words doing three jobs: downward, facing, dog pose. Say it in pieces before you say it as one.
  • Haṭha is "HUT-huh" Not "hah-tha". The h after the t is a puff of air, not a lisp.
  • Vinyāsa is "vin-YAH-suh" The stress sits on the middle syllable, not the first.
  • Śavāsana is "shuh-VAH-suh-nuh" It starts with a "sh", which is why the spelling carries an accent.

Nobody in a studio will mind if you get any of this wrong, and most teachers say these words somewhat differently from each other anyway. Being understood is the whole standard.

Fifteen words

Core vocabulary

The words that turn up in class regardless of format, style or teacher.

Āsana (AH-suh-nuh)
Seat, or pose. The ending on nearly every pose name. It originally meant a seat for sitting practice, and only later came to mean the shapes themselves.
Vinyāsa (vin-YAH-suh)
To place in a special way. In a class it means movement linked to breath, and it is also the name of a whole format.
Prāṇāyāma (prah-nah-YAH-muh)
Breathing practice. Usually rendered as extension or regulation of the breath.
Prāṇa (PRAH-nuh)
Breath, or vital energy. Which of the two a teacher means depends on the teacher and the text.
Dṛṣṭi (DRISH-tee)
Gaze point. Where the eyes settle during a pose. A steady gaze steadies balance, which is why teachers keep mentioning it.
Bandha (BAHN-dah)
Lock or bind. A deliberate engagement in one area of the body, most often the pelvic floor or the lower abdomen.
Mudrā (MOO-drah)
Seal or gesture, most often made with the hands.
Namaste (NUH-muh-stay)
A respectful greeting. Western studios commonly use it to close a class; it is less universal in India than many people assume.
Om (ohm)
A syllable chanted at the start or end of some classes. Optional everywhere. Sitting quietly through it is entirely normal.
Śavāsana (shuh-VAH-suh-nuh)
Corpse Pose. The final rest, flat on your back, doing nothing. It closes nearly every practice.
Yoga (YOH-guh)
From a root meaning to yoke or to join. In everyday use, the whole practice, of which the physical poses are one part.
Haṭha (HUT-huh)
Force or effort. Covers physical yoga practice broadly, and doubles as a common label on a studio schedule.
Mysore (MY-sore)
A city in India, and from it the name of a class format where each person works through their own portion of a set sequence with individual attention.
Sūrya Namaskāra (SOOR-yuh nuh-muh-SKAH-ruh)
Sun Salutation. A short linked sequence, repeated several times, that opens a great many classes.
Nāḍī (NAH-dee)
Channel. It appears in nāḍī śodhana, the name for alternate nostril breathing.

Words that appear inside pose names

Sanskrit pose names are descriptive compounds, not arbitrary labels. Learn these fifteen pieces and most names on a class schedule decode themselves.

Adho (AH-doh)
Downward. As in Adho Mukha Śvānāsana, downward-facing dog.
Ūrdhva (OORD-vuh)
Upward. As in Ūrdhva Mukha Śvānāsana, upward-facing dog.
Mukha (MOO-kuh)

Pāda (PAH-duh)

Śīrṣa (SHEER-shuh)

Pārśva (PAHRSH-vuh)

Utthita (oot-TEE-tuh)
Extended.
Supta (SOOP-tuh)
Reclined, or sleeping. Marks the lying-down version of a pose.
Ardha (ARD-huh)
Half. Marks a shorter or partial version.
Baddha (BAHD-huh)
Bound.
Sālamba (SAH-lum-buh)
Supported, either by a prop or by the arms.
Parivṛtta (puh-ree-VRIT-tuh)
Revolved, or twisted. Marks the twisting version of a standing pose.

Called out in class

Pose names you will hear

Twenty names a general-level class is most likely to call out, listed roughly in the order they tend to arrive.

Tāḍāsana (tah-DAH-suh-nuh)
Mountain Pose. Standing still, weight spread evenly across both feet.
Bālāsana (bah-LAH-suh-nuh)
Child's Pose. Kneeling and folded forward. Full guide.
Mārjaryāsana (mar-jar-YAH-suh-nuh)
Cat Pose. Rounding the spine on hands and knees.
Bitilāsana (bee-tee-LAH-suh-nuh)
Cow Pose. The counterpart to Cat, letting the belly descend.
Adho Mukha Śvānāsana (AH-doh MOO-kuh shvah-NAH-suh-nuh)
Downward-Facing Dog. Full guide.
Uttānāsana (oo-tah-NAH-suh-nuh)
Standing Forward Fold.
Añjaneyāsana (ahn-jah-nay-AH-suh-nuh)
Low Lunge, with the back knee down on the floor.
Vīrabhadrāsana II (veer-ah-bah-DRAH-suh-nuh)
Warrior II. Full guide.
Utthita Trikoṇāsana (oot-TEE-tuh trik-oh-NAH-suh-nuh)
Extended Triangle Pose.
Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana (oot-TEE-tuh parsh-vuh-koh-NAH-suh-nuh)
Extended Side Angle Pose.
Vṛkṣāsana (vrik-SHAH-suh-nuh)
Tree Pose. Full guide.
Utkaṭāsana (oot-kuh-TAH-suh-nuh)
Chair Pose. Sitting down onto a seat that is not there.
Phalakāsana (fuh-luh-KAH-suh-nuh)
Plank Pose.
Bhujaṅgāsana (boo-jung-GAH-suh-nuh)
Cobra Pose. Lifting the chest while lying face down.
Setu Bandha Sarvāṅgāsana (SAY-too BAHN-dah sar-vahn-GAH-suh-nuh)
Bridge Pose. Full guide.
Paścimottānāsana (POSH-chee-moh-tah-NAH-suh-nuh)
Seated Forward Fold. Full guide.
Jānu Śīrṣāsana (JAH-noo sheer-SHAH-suh-nuh)
Head-to-Knee Forward Bend.
Baddha Koṇāsana (BAHD-huh koh-NAH-suh-nuh)
Bound Angle Pose. Soles of the feet together, knees wide.
Ardha Matsyendrāsana (ARD-huh mots-yen-DRAH-suh-nuh)
Half Lord of the Fishes. The standard seated twist.
Viparīta Karaṇī (vip-uh-REE-tuh kuh-ruh-NEE)
Legs Up the Wall. Commonly held for five to fifteen minutes.

English terms used on this site

Plain English, not Sanskrit, but worth pinning down because other sources use several of these words to mean different things.

Alignment cue
A specific instruction about where a body part goes or what it does once you are in a pose. Different from an entry step, which is about getting into the shape at all.
Hold time
How long you stay in a pose. Given here in seconds or in breaths, never as "a few breaths".
Contraindication
A reason to approach a pose carefully or leave it out. Every pose guide on this site carries one.
Modification
A version of a pose set up differently, usually with a prop. Written here as an equal alternative rather than a lesser one.
Progression
The next step from a pose once the current version is steady. Usually more time rather than more depth.
Counter-pose
A shape practiced after a strong one to move the spine or joints the other way. Child's Pose after a backbend is the standard example.
Prop
Anything that brings the floor closer, makes your arms longer, or carries your weight. Blocks, straps, blankets, bolsters, chairs and walls.
Restorative
A format built on heavy prop support and holds of five to twenty minutes, with no muscular effort at all. More on formats.
Flow
Informal name for a Vinyasa class, or for any linked run of poses inside a class.
Level one
A studio label for a class suitable for beginners. Also written as fundamentals, foundations, gentle or all levels.

Not definitions

Words teachers use that mean nothing precise

Some class language is descriptive rather than instructional. None of the phrases below carry a fixed meaning, and none of them are wrong to say. They are just not instructions, and standing in a pose trying to obey them is a way to spend an hour confused. Here is what each one is usually reaching for.

"Open your hips"
Not a thing hips do. Usually it means take the thighs further apart, or turn the thigh bones outward, or stay put and let the shape settle. Ask which, or watch the teacher's demonstration.
"Engage your core"
Too broad to act on. In practice it means firm the lower abdomen slightly, or stop your lower back arching. Pick one and see whether the pose steadies.
"Breathe into your hamstring"
Air does not go there. It means keep breathing evenly rather than holding your breath while the sensation is strong.
"Find your edge"
A useful idea with no number attached. Take it as: go to where you feel a clear stretch and can still breathe in an even four-count. If the count shortens, come back.
"Soften"
Usually means release something you are gripping without knowing it: the jaw, the shoulders, the toes, the fingers.
"Lengthen your spine"
Reasonable as an intention, unmeasurable as an instruction. It generally means stop rounding the upper back and lift the crown of the head away from the shoulders.
"Listen to your body"
True and unhelpful on day one. Concretely: stop if it is sharp, hot, or shooting, and stay if it is a broad dull stretch that eases as you breathe.
"Set an intention"
An invitation, not a task. No answer is required. Sitting there breathing is a complete response.
"Detox" or "release toxins"
No pose does this, and no teacher can make it happen. Twisting feels good and it does not clean anything.
"Advanced"
Usually means a harder shape, which is a different thing from a better practice. A long steady hold in a simple pose is not the beginner version of anything.

If a cue cannot be turned into something your body does, it is a description, not an instruction. Ask for the instruction.

Why the diacritics

A note on the language

Sanskrit pose names are not decoration and they are not a barrier put up to keep people out. They describe what is happening. Utthita means extended, pārśva means side, koṇa means angle, so Utthita Pārśvakoṇāsana is extended side angle pose. Once fifteen or so of these pieces are familiar, most names on a schedule read themselves.

Writing the marks is accuracy rather than ceremony. The line over the ā and the dot under the ṣ belong to the spelling in the same way an accent belongs to the spelling of a French word. Plenty of reliable sources leave them off. This site does not, which is why some spellings here look unfamiliar next to spellings elsewhere.
