Child's Pose

Bālāsana  (BAH-lah-suh-nuh)

You kneel, send your hips back toward your heels, and fold forward until your forehead has something to land on. Bāla means child. This is the shape a teacher points to when they say take a rest, and it is the one most people set up carelessly, which is why so few of them find it restful.

The whole pose

Set-up, cues and every version

Bālāsana

  • Beginner
  • Hips
  • Spine
  • No prop required
Difficulty
Beginner. What you can do here is decided by your knees and ankles, not by strength.
Breath pattern
Long exhale. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 8 counts, through the nose.
Suggested hold
1 to 3 minutes on the floor. Longer once a bolster is carrying you.
What moves
Flexion at the hip and the knee, length across the front of the ankle, lengthening down the back of the spine
Props that help
A bolster, one or two blankets, a block, a chair or a table edge
Where it appears
10-Minute Morning, Evening Wind-Down, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Kneel down with your shins flat on the mat and the tops of your feet on the floor.
  • Bring your big toes to touch behind you, so the heels angle slightly apart.
  • Pick a knee width. Knees together narrows the shape and presses on the belly. Knees out toward the edges of the mat leaves the front of your body room, and suits most people better.
  • Travel your hips back toward your heels, as far as they comfortably go. They do not have to arrive.
  • Walk your hands forward along the mat and lower your forehead onto the floor, a stacked fist, a block or a bolster.
  • Pick an arm position. Reaching forward lengthens the sides of the ribs. Arms back alongside the body, palms up, releases more across the shoulders.
  • Drop the shoulders, unclench the jaw, and let the exhale stretch out to eight counts.
Alignment cues
  • Land the forehead on something. If the floor is too far away, bring the floor up with a block or a folded blanket. A head left hanging keeps the back of your neck working for the entire hold.
  • Send the sit bones back, not the chest down. The direction of this pose is backward. Pressing the ribs toward the mat only arches the lower back.
  • Tops of the feet flat, ankles long. If the front of an ankle objects, roll a blanket and slide it underneath.
  • Shoulders away from the ears in either arm position. If they creep up, walk the hands wider.
  • Let the breath go into your back. The front of your body is folded shut, so the ribs expand backward. You will feel the movement between your shoulder blades.
  • Nothing gripping, anywhere. If you can feel a muscle holding you in the shape, that is the spot that needs a prop under it.
Common mistakes
  • Dragging the hips down to the heels. They sit where your hip and knee proportions let them sit. Forcing it peels the knees off the mat and loads the knee joint sideways.
  • Leaving the forehead in mid-air. The most common error on this page, and the reason people report that Child's Pose is not restful. Raise the floor to meet your head.
  • Knees too narrow for your build. If you feel squeezed at the front of the hips or across the belly, take the knees wider. There is no correct width, only yours.
  • Putting up with the ankles. Discomfort across the front of the ankle is common and takes one rolled blanket to solve. Solve it.
  • Passing through instead of staying. One to three minutes of eight-count exhales is the pose. Two seconds on the way to the next thing is a transition.
  • Shoulders riding up toward the ears. Reaching arms plus narrow hands does this. Walk the hands wider apart, or lay the arms back alongside your body.
Accessible modifications

Every one of these is the pose, not a discount version of it. The bolster set-up is what long-time practitioners reach for when they intend to stay five minutes.

Over a bolster

Lay a bolster lengthwise between your knees, one short end close to your hips, then let your whole torso down onto it and turn your head to one side. Swap which way the head faces halfway through so the neck works evenly. Because the bolster carries the full weight of your torso, five to ten minutes becomes possible. Two firm pillows stacked inside one pillowcase do the same job.

Blanket behind the knees

Before you sit back, roll a blanket and tuck it into the crease behind both knees. It limits how far the knee has to fold, which is what puts this pose back within reach for a great many people. Add a second roll across the ankles if the front of the ankle is the part complaining.

Block under the forehead

Stand a block on any of its three heights directly under your forehead. An adult head weighs somewhere around ten pounds, and handing that weight to a block is the whole difference between resting and holding a position. Your own stacked fists, or a folded hand towel, work just as well.

Seated at a chair or table

If kneeling is off the table, sit on a chair with both feet flat, put a cushion on your lap or a folded blanket on a table in front of you, hinge forward and rest your forehead on it with your arms hanging loose. Same fold, same eight-count exhale, same rest, and nothing at all asked of the knees or ankles.

Progression

You do not get better at this pose by going deeper. You get better at staying. Build from one minute to three on the floor, then from three to five with a bolster underneath you. Once five minutes is genuinely easy, walk both hands over to the left and stay ninety seconds, which lengthens the right side of the ribs, then change. After that, Sphinx Pose or a supported forward fold held for a similar stretch of time is the sensible next thing to learn.

Contraindications and cautions

Take care, or take the chair version, if you have a knee injury or a recent knee replacement, an ankle injury, or a hip injury or replacement that restricts deep flexion. In pregnancy, use the wide-knee version over a bolster so the front of your body has room. After recent abdominal surgery, use the seated version instead. Come out if you feel sharp sensation in a knee, pins and needles in the feet, or pressure building in your head.

Calibration

What this should feel like, and what it should not

Run through this once at around the thirty-second mark. Fix whatever is on the right-hand list, then stop auditing yourself and breathe.

Signs it is set up well

A broad, dull stretch low in the back and around the outer hips. Even pressure across your forehead and the front of your shins. A mild pull along the tops of both feet. Movement you can feel between your shoulder blades as you inhale. Your exhale lengthening on its own, without you managing it. Arms that are genuinely doing nothing.

Signs to change something

Sharp or pinching sensation inside a knee: roll a blanket behind the knees. Pins and needles in the feet: come up. Pressure behind the eyes or in the sinuses: raise the forehead higher. A neck that is working: your head is hanging, so give it a block. Any muscle you can feel gripping: put a prop under that spot.

  • Breath Inhale 4 counts, exhale 8, through the nose.
  • Hold One to three minutes on the floor. Five or more over a bolster.
  • Prop None required, but a block under the forehead changes the most for the least effort.

If you can feel a muscle holding you in the shape, the shape is not resting you.

The one test that fixes most uncomfortable Child's Pose

Rest, practiced

The pose that gets offered and declined

In a group class this shape is offered roughly every ten minutes and taken by almost nobody. Part of that is social. Sitting out feels like being watched sitting out, even when nobody is looking.

The rest of it is set-up. Nearly every person who tells us Child's Pose is uncomfortable is doing one of three specific things: kneeling with the forehead hanging in mid-air, holding the knees closer together than their hips want, or asking a knee to fold further than it currently folds. A block, a wider stance and a rolled blanket answer all three, and each takes about four seconds.

Once it is set up honestly, this is a shape you can stay inside for five minutes with an eight-count exhale. That is a different experience from the fifteen-second version snatched between rounds of something harder. If you are building a practice at home, give this one real minutes.
