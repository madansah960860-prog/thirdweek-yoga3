Eight to forty minutes

Four sequences, timed

One of these runs eight minutes in a desk chair. One runs forty minutes on a mat. All four tell you the order of the poses, how many seconds you stay in each one, what to have within reach before you begin, and how to do every shape with a chair or a wall instead. The page keeps time so you do not have to watch a clock, and it prints onto paper if you would rather practice without a screen in the room.

10-Minute Morning

  • 10 minutes
  • Beginner
  • Mat, wall

Ten poses, ten minutes, finished before the kettle boils. The spine moves in every direction it has, the front of the hips opens after a night flat on a bed, and weight goes back through the hands and feet. A chair version runs beside it, pose for pose.

Desk Reset

  • 8 minutes
  • Beginner
  • Chair only

Twelve movements from a chair and beside a desk. No mat, no floor, no change of clothes, nothing that looks like exercise to the person across the room. It is built for the part of a working day when you have eight minutes and nowhere private to spend them.

Evening Wind-Down

  • 20 minutes
  • Beginner
  • Bolster, block, wall

Ten supported holds of one to three minutes, all of them on the floor. Nothing asks for strength and nothing asks for balance. The exhale runs twice as long as the inhale from the first pose to the last, and it ends with your legs resting up a wall.

Gentle Full Practice

  • 40 minutes
  • Beginner to intermediate
  • Mat, blocks, strap, blanket

Twenty-two entries: warm-up, standing work, floor work, then five minutes lying still. It is the shape of a studio class without the drive there, paced so you can set a pose up properly instead of landing in it and hoping.

01 / Choosing

A short decision guide

Which sequence should you pick today?

Choose by the time you have and the room you are standing in, not by how ambitious you feel at the moment of choosing. Find the line below that describes your afternoon and follow it.

  • You are at a desk, in work clothes
    Desk Reset
    Nothing in it touches the floor and nothing needs a mat. Two of the twelve movements are done standing at the desk and look like stretching, because that is what they are.
    • 8 minutes
  • You are awake, stiff, and short of time
    10-Minute Morning
    Ten poses that move the spine in every direction before the day asks you to sit in one. It needs a wall and about the floor space of a bath towel.
    • 10 minutes
  • You are tired and do not want to work
    Evening Wind-Down
    Every hold is propped so that no muscle has to keep you there. Pick this one when the thought of standing up on one leg is the reason you are about to skip practicing at all.
    • 20 minutes
  • You have a clear hour and no one waiting
    Gentle Full Practice
    The only sequence here with a full arc: warm-up, standing work, floor work, rest. It expects you to have met Downward-Facing Dog and Warrior II somewhere first.
    • 40 minutes
  • Two sequences fit Take the shorter one. You can repeat it tomorrow.
  • None of them fit Do the first four poses of any of them and stop there.
  • You cannot decide Start with the ten-minute morning. It is the one the other three assume you have met.

The forty-minute practice you keep postponing teaches you nothing. The eight-minute one you actually do teaches you something every time.

02 / Construction

Six build rules

How these sequences are built

The same six rules shape every sequence here. They are worth knowing even if you never practice one of ours, because they are what to look for in anyone else's.

  • Warm first, load second. Nothing in the opening two minutes asks for range you may not have yet. The spine moves before it is asked to hold anything.
  • Both sides, matched. Every asymmetrical pose is done on the right and the left for the same number of seconds, in the same order, every time through.
  • The hold time is a number. "A few breaths" means eight seconds to one person and forty to another. Each pose here carries a figure you can check against a clock.
  • Strong shapes get an answer. A backbend is followed by something neutral or gently rounded. Two backbends never sit next to each other.
  • The last thing is rest. Every sequence finishes lying down or sitting still, for thirty seconds at the shortest and five minutes at the longest.
  • The chair path is written out. Not a note telling you to modify, but a named substitute for each pose, with the same timing and the same place in the order.

On-page timer

Using the timer

The timer at the top of each sequence counts down one hold, moves to the next pose on its own, and tells you which pose you are on and how many are left. Pause it whenever you need to. Step forward and back with the two buttons. Tap any row in the order below it to send the timer straight to that pose, which is what you want when you decide to repeat a side or walk past a shape that is not for you today.

Treat the number as a floor you may leave early and a ceiling you may pass. Come out the moment something hurts, no matter what the clock says. Stay longer when the pose is comfortable and your breath has not changed.

Printing a sequence

Use your browser's print command on any sequence page. What comes off the printer is the pose order, the hold times, the prop list, the chair path and the safety note. The header, the navigation, the timer buttons, the advertisement and the closing commentary are all left behind. That is two or three sheets of letter paper, set large enough to read from standing.

Health and safety

These sequences are general movement education. They are not medical advice, and nothing here treats, prevents or manages any health condition. Talk to a qualified healthcare professional before you start a new physical practice, and particularly if you are pregnant, recently injured, post-surgical or managing a health condition. If a shape hurts, come out of it. For hands-on correction, work with a qualified teacher in a room with you. Read the full disclaimer.
