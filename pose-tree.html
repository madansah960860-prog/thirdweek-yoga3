Standing balance

Tree Pose

Vṛkṣāsana  (vrik-SHAH-suh-nuh)

You stand on one leg and press the other foot into your ankle, calf or inner thigh. Vṛkṣa means tree. More people tell us they cannot do this pose than any other on the site, and almost all of them are attempting it in the middle of an empty floor. Stand next to a wall and the difficulty changes character entirely.

The whole pose

Set-up, cues and every version

  • Beginner

Difficulty
Beginner beside a wall. Intermediate once you step away from it.
Breath pattern
Even. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts. When the breath steadies, so does the leg.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths a side, which works out around 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
The small stabilizers of the foot and ankle, the hip abductors on the standing side, external rotation at the hip on the lifted side
Props that help
A wall, the back of a sturdy chair, a block wedged between foot and leg
Where it appears
10-Minute Morning, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Stand with your feet hip-width apart and spread all ten toes against the floor.
  • Move your weight over into your left foot, and watch that your left hip does not swing out sideways as you do it.
  • Turn your right knee out to the side and bend it.
  • Set the sole of your right foot on your left ankle, your left calf, or your left inner thigh. Never on the side of the knee joint.
  • Press the foot into the leg and the leg back into the foot with matching pressure, so the two hold each other in place.
  • Draw your right hip down until both hip points sit level and keep facing forward.
  • Bring your hands together in front of your chest. Reach them overhead only if the legs are already steady.
  • Find something stationary at about eye height, a few feet ahead of you, and keep your eyes on it.
Alignment cues
  • Never on the knee. The lifted foot goes above the standing knee or below it, never against the side of it. This is the one rule on the page with no exceptions.
  • Both directions of pressure. Foot into leg, leg into foot. Without that, the foot slides down and you spend the hold fighting it rather than standing.
  • Hips level. Your standing hip wants to jut out to the side. Draw the lifted hip down and the standing hip back in until the two points match.
  • Standing thigh awake, knee not locked. Switch the thigh on, but leave a millimetre of bend at the knee. A locked knee is rigid, and rigid is not the same as stable.
  • Three points under the standing foot. Share the weight between the base of the big toe, the base of the little toe and the middle of the heel.
  • Give your eyes one job. Pick something that does not move and stay with it. Balance falls apart measurably the moment the gaze wanders.
Common mistakes
  • Parking the foot on the knee. It loads a joint sideways in a direction that joint does not travel. Move the foot down to the ankle or up to the thigh.
  • Letting the standing hip jut out. It bends your whole spine into an S and makes balancing harder, not easier. Level the two hip points and the pose quietens down immediately.
  • Holding your breath. Near universal in balance poses, and it works against you: breath-holding degrades the very corrections you are relying on. If the breath stops, lower the foot.
  • Reading a wobble as failure. The wobble is your ankle stabilizers doing their job out loud. It is the training itself, not evidence against you.
  • Setting up in the middle of the room. There is nothing to be gained by it. Stand beside a wall for the first few weeks and you will spend that time practicing rather than catching yourself.
  • Arms overhead too early. Lifting the arms raises your centre of mass and makes the whole thing harder. Keep the hands at the chest until the legs are boring.
Accessible modifications

All four are the pose. None of them is the runner-up version, and two of them are places you might reasonably stay for good.

Fingertips on a wall

Stand with a wall about a forearm's length to your side and rest two or three fingertips against it. That is enough contact to give your nervous system a fixed reference. It takes away the fear of falling, which is very often the thing actually making this pose difficult, and it frees up the whole hold for working on level hips.

Toes on the floor, heel on the ankle

Leave the toes of the lifted foot on the floor beside your standing foot and rest only the heel against the standing ankle. Think of it as a kickstand. You still get the rotation at the hip and the shift of weight, but you keep a second point of contact with the ground. For anyone with an unstable ankle, this is a permanent home, not a stage.

Holding a chair back

Stand behind a sturdy chair and take hold of the back with one hand or both. Worth choosing if you are managing any dizziness, if the floor underfoot is uneven, or if you simply want two uninterrupted minutes to study what your standing hip does when it gets tired.

Seated version

Sit tall on a firm chair with both feet flat. Cross your right ankle over your left thigh, let the right knee open out to the side, and press that knee gently away while keeping your spine long. Same external rotation at the hip, same two-way pressure, and no balance asked of you at all.

Progression

When you can hold eight breaths a side beside the wall without actually touching it, take one step away and repeat, hands still at the chest. Once that is steady, add the arms overhead. From there the useful progression is duration rather than difficulty: build toward two minutes a side. If you want more, close one eye, then both. Removing the visual reference hands the entire job to your ankle and hip, and it is humbling.

Contraindications and cautions

Use the wall, the chair or the seated version if you get dizziness or vertigo, have low blood pressure, are recovering from an inner ear condition, have an unstable ankle or knee, or have had a recent hip or knee replacement. The same goes in pregnancy, when your centre of gravity moves week by week. Stand on a firm level surface rather than thick carpet or an uneven floor. Come out if you feel sharp sensation in the standing knee or the outer hip.

The small version

If you only have sixty seconds

This is the one pose on the site that fits into an ordinary day without ceremony. You need bare feet, a firm floor and something within arm's reach. Thirty seconds a side, most days, does more for your ankles than one long session a week.

  1. Seconds 0 to 5: set the standing foot

    Bare feet, near a counter or a doorframe. Spread all ten toes, then move your weight into the left foot and feel for the three points: big toe base, little toe base, middle of the heel.

  2. Seconds 5 to 10: place the foot, level the hips

    Right foot to the ankle or the inner thigh, never the side of the knee. Press foot and leg into each other. Then draw the right hip down until both hip points match. That last part is the bit everyone skips.

  3. Seconds 10 to 30: breathe, and let it wobble

    Hands at the chest. Eyes on something that does not move. Four counts in, four counts out, roughly four breaths. If the breath stops, put the foot down. The wobbling is the training, so leave it alone.

  4. Seconds 30 to 60: the second side

    Lower the foot with control rather than dropping it, then repeat on the other leg. Expect the two sides to feel different. That difference is useful information, not a problem to fix today.

  • Breath Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4, through the nose.
  • Hold Five to eight breaths a side, or thirty seconds if you are counting time.
  • Prop A wall within fingertip reach. It is the difference between practising and catching yourself.

A trainable skill

Balance is something you practise, not something you have

Most people turn up to this pose already holding a verdict about whether they are good at balancing. Press on it and the verdict usually turns out to be decades old and never retested.

Standing on one leg is a job your nervous system does by making constant small corrections, using information from the sole of your foot, from your inner ear, and from your eyes. All three of those sharpen with practice, and they sharpen faster than you would guess. Thirty seconds a side, most days, for a fortnight, makes a difference you can feel in how much correcting your ankle has to do.

What does not help is treating every wobble as evidence. The wobble is the work. Someone standing perfectly still in this shape is not balancing better than someone wobbling next to them; they are further along the same process, and they got there by wobbling.

It also swings day to day, more than you would expect. Tiredness, a long drive, new shoes, and the surface under your feet all change it. A wobbly Tuesday after a steady Monday tells you nothing at all.

The wobble is not the pose going wrong. The wobble is the pose working.
