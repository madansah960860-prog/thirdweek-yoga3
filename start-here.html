Kit and props

What you actually need

For the first few weeks, nothing. A firm floor, room to lie down with your arms out to the sides, and a chair that does not roll or swivel will get you through a month. Carpet is fine. A rug over hardwood is fine. A cleared strip of bedroom floor between the bed and the wall is fine.

When you do buy something, buy a mat first. A mat gives you an edge to work inside, stops your hands sliding on a wood floor, and puts the same surface under your knees every time. The standard is 68 inches long and 24 inches wide. Above roughly 5 feet 10 inches, look for 72 or 84 inches so your head and your heels are both still on it when you lie down.

Everything after the mat, in the order it earns its place:

  • Two blocks. Think of a block as the floor arriving sooner. It goes under a hand in a standing pose, under the sitting bones on the floor, or under the forehead in a fold. Cork is heavier and steadier; foam is lighter and quieter on a wood floor. Standard size is 9 by 6 by 4 inches. Owning two cheap ones beats owning one good one.
  • One strap. A strap is a longer arm. Loop it around a foot and you can hold a leg without dragging your spine forward to reach it. Six feet suits most people. Eight feet suits taller bodies and anything that passes the strap behind your back. A bathrobe belt or a luggage strap does the identical job for nothing.
  • One or two firm blankets. Folded into a stack, a blanket lifts the hips in seated poses so your lower back stops rounding. It also pads a knee, and it goes over you in the final rest, when your body temperature drops further than you expect.
  • A bolster, if slow supported practice is what keeps pulling you back. It is a firm cylinder or rectangle built to take the whole weight of your torso. Two dense bed pillows stuffed into one pillowcase is a genuine substitute, not a compromise.

What to wear

Something you can bend, kneel and lie down in that will not fall over your head when you tip forward. That is the whole specification, and you almost certainly own it. Snug at the waist and the ankle is the part that matters; snug everywhere is a preference, not a requirement.

Go barefoot if you can. Skin grips better than cotton, and your toes are doing real work in every standing pose. If bare feet are uncomfortable or not an option, socks with rubber dots across the sole solve it, and they cost a few dollars anywhere.

Take off anything that will dig in when you lie on it. Belts, watches, hard clips, long necklaces and the back pocket of a pair of jeans all make themselves known about thirty seconds into the first floor pose.

How long and how often

Ten to twenty minutes, three or four days a week. That is a realistic first month for most adults, and it is deliberately modest. How often you practice matters more than how long, because the skill you are building early is knowing where your limbs are, and that comes from repetition. Four ten-minute practices spread across a week beat one forty-minute effort on a Sunday, and you are far more likely to still be doing them in March.

A shape for the week that works:

  • Two short mornings. The ten-minute morning sequence was built for exactly this slot and needs nothing but a wall.
  • One or two breaks in the working day. The eight-minute desk reset runs from an office chair, in office clothes, shoes on.
  • One longer evening. The twenty-minute evening wind-down stays on the floor throughout, with holds long enough that you stop counting.

Once those feel ordinary rather than novel, the forty-minute gentle full practice is where to go next. There is no hurry to arrive there.

Why every pose has two names

Almost every pose on this site is listed twice, once in English and once in Sanskrit. Sanskrit is the classical language the older yoga texts were written in, and the pose names are plain description rather than ceremony. Āsana means seat or pose, which is why it ends nearly all of them. Adho mukha means downward-facing. Śvāna means dog. Put those together and Adho Mukha Śvānāsana says, literally, downward-facing dog pose.

None of this is required in order to practice. It becomes useful in a studio, where a teacher may name a pose in Sanskrit and move straight on. Learn the building blocks rather than the vocabulary: knowing that eka pāda means one-foot decodes a dozen names at once. The glossary holds sixty terms with a plain-English pronunciation beside each one.

We write the diacritics because they are part of the word. The bar over the ā in āsana marks a long vowel, held about twice as long as a short one. Leaving it off changes the word, the same way dropping the accent changes a French one.

What the difficulty labels mean

A difficulty label here describes the pose, not you. It records how much coordination the shape asks for and how much familiarity it assumes you already have. It is a note about the instructions, not a grade.

  • Beginner. You can get into it from a written description on the first attempt. Either the balance demand is low or a wall is written into the instructions. Every one has a chair or floor version.
  • Intermediate. Two or three things have to happen at the same time, or you have to keep breathing evenly while holding a position that would rather you did not. These get much easier once the beginner poses underneath them are familiar.
  • Advanced. Range, strength or balance that most bodies build across months or years, and better learned with somebody watching you. We label these honestly and we do not nudge anyone toward them.

Beginner does not mean lesser. Child's Pose is labeled beginner, and it is practiced daily by people who have been on a mat for thirty years.

Breathing while you move

The default is short enough to remember on the floor: in and out through the nose, evenly, without ever holding your breath. If you notice you are holding it, the pose is asking for more than you have today. The fix is not to push harder. The fix is to make the pose smaller until the breath comes back.

Two patterns cover nearly everything you will meet here:

  • Even breath. In for four counts, out for four. This is the one for standing poses and anything you are holding under effort.
  • Extended exhale. In for four, out for six or eight. This is the one for supported shapes, lying-down poses and the final rest. The long half is the exhale, every time.

The narrow bar under the header on every page will count one of these for you. Press Start the pacer and it fills while you breathe in, empties while you breathe out. On a sequence page it carries that sequence's own pattern rather than the site default. Breathwork Basics goes through five patterns in detail, including the signs that mean stop.

Telling sensation, effort and pain apart

This is the single most useful thing you will learn in a first year, and it is worth reading twice. Three different things happen in a pose, they feel superficially similar at the start, and only one of them means stop.

  • Sensation is broad and dull, spread across the belly of a muscle rather than concentrated at a point. It settles as you keep breathing, and it is gone a breath or two after you come out.
  • Effort is work. The front leg shakes in a lunge, your breath tries to get shorter, and you can feel yourself starting to negotiate. Normal, and the correct response is a shorter hold, not a clenched jaw.
  • Pain is sharp, hot, electrical or pinching, and it usually lives in a joint rather than a muscle. Breathing does not soften it. It is information, not a test of character.

Come out of anything that hurts. This is not a beginner's allowance that you graduate out of; it is what experienced practitioners do, and it is the only reason some of them are still practicing at seventy.

What nobody tells beginners

None of what follows appears in a class description, and all of it comes up in the first month. Knowing it in advance removes most of the reason people quit in week two.

  • It feels silly Kneeling on a floor in your own living room is a strange thing to do for the first week. The feeling passes around session four.
  • Your foot will cramp Usually the arch, usually in a kneeling pose. Stand up, put the foot flat on the floor, carry on. It stops happening within a month.
  • Everyone wobbles Balance is not a fixed trait. It varies with sleep, caffeine, and which foot you are on. A wobbly Tree is a working Tree.
  • Nobody is watching In a class, every other person is fully occupied by their own hamstrings. This is the most reliable fact in the room.

Two more that matter more than they sound. First, flexibility is not the entry requirement, it is one of the things practice slowly produces, and hamstrings in particular take months rather than weeks. Second, the final rest is not the optional part. Lying still for five minutes at the end is where the nervous system registers what just happened, and it is the piece beginners skip first and miss most.

You are not behind. There is nothing to be behind on, because nobody is going anywhere.

Four week plan

Your first four weeks

Here is a month that has worked for a lot of people. It assumes nothing about your current range of motion, it needs a chair and a wall, and it never asks for more than twenty minutes in a day.

  1. Week one: get on the floor

    Three runs of the ten-minute morning sequence, and nothing else. Do not add poses, do not look up anything new. The single goal this week is that getting on the floor stops being an occasion. Expect it to feel clumsy. Clumsy is what week one is.

  2. Week two: add the desk reset

    Keep the same three mornings and add two eight-minute desk resets somewhere in the working day. This is the week the practice stops competing with your calendar and starts fitting inside it, which is the thing that decides whether it survives.

  3. Week three: read one pose properly

    Same schedule, plus twenty minutes of reading. Pick the pose you keep running into, open its full guide, and read the alignment cues and common mistakes before you next do it. Almost everyone finds at least one thing they have been doing for a fortnight without noticing.

  4. Week four: add one long practice

    Everything from week three, plus one longer session on a day nobody is waiting for you. Either the twenty-minute evening wind-down or the forty-minute gentle practice. Then run the whole month again from week one. The repeat is not a placeholder. The repeat is the method.

Tonight, if you want

Your first ten minutes, minute by minute

If you would rather begin than read any further, this is a complete first practice. It needs a floor, a wall and about ten minutes. Nothing in it requires a mat, and every shape has a chair version in the library if the floor is not available to you today.

Minutes 0 to 2
Lie on your back, knees bent, feet on the floor. Hand on the ribs. In for four, out for six. Ten rounds.
Minutes 2 to 4
Knees to one side, then the other. A minute each way. Let the floor take the weight of the legs.
Minutes 4 to 6
Child's Pose, forehead on the floor or on a stacked fist. Hips travel back as far as they travel. They do not have to arrive.
Minutes 6 to 8
Stand. Feet hip width. Slow forward fold with soft knees, then roll up. Three times, following the breath.
Minutes 8 to 10
Back down. Legs up the wall, or knees bent on the floor. Stay still. This is the part people skip.

That is a practice. It is not a warm-up for a practice. If ten minutes is what you have, ten minutes done four times this week will teach you more than an hour you keep postponing until Sunday. The ten-minute morning sequence is the fuller written version of the same idea, with hold times and a chair column.

In the room

Your first studio class

Sooner or later most people want a teacher present, and they are right to. A website cannot see you. A teacher standing three feet away can see the one thing you have no access to, which is your own body from the outside.

What to know before you walk in:

  • Get there ten minutes early and say it is your first class. Teachers want to know, and not one of them will mind being told.
  • Mention what changes their instructions. Recent surgery, a pregnancy, a knee that objects to kneeling. You owe nobody a medical history, only the part that affects what they should offer you.
  • Read the label on the timetable. Beginner, fundamentals, gentle, level one and all levels are all fine for a first visit. Level two, power and led primary series are not, whatever the description promises.
  • Resting is part of the class, not a failure of it. Dropping into Child's Pose for a round happens in every room, including among people who have practiced for fifteen years.
  • Settle hands-on adjustments before it starts. Many studios hand out consent cards. If yours does not, "no hands-on today, thanks" is a complete sentence and needs no explanation after it.
