Breath practice

Five breathing patterns, counted out

Five patterns you will meet in a yoga class, each with its count, its setup, how long to stay with it and the point at which you stop. This is technique written plainly. It makes no claim about what breathing does for anything except where your attention goes.

The five patterns

Read this first

Changing how you breathe changes how you feel, and not everyone finds that pleasant. What follows is technique and nothing more. Nothing on this page treats, prevents or improves any health condition, and we are not claiming otherwise.

Stop immediately if you feel dizzy, lightheaded, short of breath, tingling in the hands or face, anxious, or if your vision changes. Go back to ordinary breathing that you are not managing, and stay seated until it settles. Speak with a qualified healthcare professional before practicing breathing techniques if you are pregnant, have a respiratory or cardiovascular condition, high or low blood pressure, a history of fainting, a history of panic, or a seizure disorder. Full health and safety disclaimer.

One setup, all five patterns

Each pattern below starts from the same position, and getting that position right removes most of the difficulty before you begin.

  • Sit so your spine stays upright without effort. A chair with both feet flat on the floor is a perfectly good answer and is often better than sitting on the floor. If you do sit cross-legged, perch on the front edge of a folded blanket so your hips end up higher than your knees.
  • Leave the ribs room to move. Slumping shuts the front of the ribcage and makes every pattern harder. Holding yourself rigidly upright does the same damage from the other direction.
  • Breathe in and out through the nose, unless a pattern tells you otherwise.
  • Set a timer so you are not opening one eye to check a clock. Two to five minutes covers anything on this page.
  • Count at your own speed. A count is a unit, not a second. Find a tempo where four counts is a comfortable inhale for your body, then hold that tempo steady.

The slim bar under the site header counts these patterns for you. On this page it is set to box breathing, four counts in every phase. Press Start the pacer and follow the fill. If reduced motion is switched on in your operating system, the bar prints the pattern as text and stays still.

A count is a unit, not a second. Pick a tempo your body agrees with and keep it.

The one instruction that fixes most beginner breathwork

1. Even breath

Sanskrit
Sama Vṛtti, meaning equal fluctuation
Count
Inhale 4, exhale 4
Duration
2 to 5 minutes, or throughout a standing pose
Where it is used
Standing poses, balance work, the whole morning sequence

This is the baseline. Four counts in, four counts out, through the nose, no pause at either end. That is the technique in full.

What makes it worth doing is that it hands you something exact to track while you are holding a shape, and it makes the moment your breath goes ragged impossible to miss. In a standing pose, a count that starts shortening is the first honest sign that you have gone further than today allows, and it shows up long before anything starts to hurt.

Use three counts if four feels hurried. Use five if four feels stretched. The two halves matching is what matters; the number itself does not.

2. Extended exhale

Sanskrit
Viṣama Vṛtti, meaning unequal fluctuation
Count
Inhale 4, exhale 6 or 8
Duration
3 to 10 minutes
Where it is used
Supported poses, the evening sequence, final rest

Four counts in, six counts out. Once six feels easy, try eight. No hold at either end.

Nearly everyone finds the first few attempts harder than the description suggests, because a long exhale has to be rationed from the first count rather than spent in the first two. The usual mistake is releasing most of the air quickly and then squeezing the last of it out, which turns the final third into a strain. Let it leave slowly and at one steady rate the whole way.

Learn it lying down before you try it sitting up. Pacing an exhale is easier when you are not also keeping yourself vertical.

3. Box breathing

Also called
Square breathing, four-part breath
Count
Inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4
Duration
2 to 4 minutes to begin with
Where it is used
Seated practice, before a long hold, as a standalone practice

Four counts breathing in, four counts holding with the lungs full, four counts breathing out, four counts holding with the lungs empty. Then round again.

The two pauses are what separate this from the first two patterns, and they are also the reason to treat it carefully. Holding your breath is a real physiological event, not a mental exercise. Begin at two minutes rather than ten, and cut the holds to two counts if four feels like effort. Any urgency to breathe during a hold means the pattern is too long for you today. That is information, not a failure.

Do not run this one while driving, in water, or standing on one leg.

4. Ocean breath

Sanskrit
Ujjāyī, often translated as victorious breath
Count
Even, 4 in and 4 out, with a slight constriction at the throat
Duration
Throughout a moving practice, or 3 to 5 minutes seated
Where it is used
Vinyasa and Ashtanga classes, moving practice generally

This is the audible breath you hear filling a flowing class. The throat narrows a little so the air makes a soft sound going past, somewhere between a distant wave and the noise of fogging a window with your mouth shut.

To find it: open your mouth and breathe out as if fogging a mirror. Notice what happens at the back of your throat. Now close your mouth and make that same shape while the air leaves through your nose. Once the exhale works, bring the same slight narrowing to the inhale.

Keep it quiet enough that you are the only one who hears it clearly. If it carries across a room, it is being forced. It should never involve gripping in the jaw, the tongue or the face.

Its real use in a moving class is timing. An audible breath makes it obvious the moment you start moving faster than you are breathing.

5. Alternate nostril breathing

Sanskrit
Nāḍī Śodhana
Count
Inhale 4 one side, exhale 4 the other, alternating
Duration
2 to 5 minutes
Where it is used
Seated practice, usually at the start or end of a session

Sit tall. Your left hand can rest wherever it is comfortable. With the right hand, bring the index and middle fingers to the space between your eyebrows, which leaves the thumb free on one side and the ring finger free on the other.

  • Close the right nostril with the thumb. Breathe in through the left for four counts.
  • Close the left nostril with the ring finger and lift the thumb. Breathe out through the right for four counts.
  • Breathe in through the right for four counts.
  • Close the right, release the left. Breathe out through the left for four counts.
  • That is one round. Keep going for two to five minutes, and finish on an exhale through the left.

Touch your nose lightly. The fingers work as a valve, not a clamp, and pressing hard is the mistake almost everyone makes first. Leave this pattern alone entirely while you have a cold, blocked sinuses or a deviated septum, because one blocked side turns the whole thing into a fight.

Knowing when to skip it

When not to do breathwork

Most breathing practice is unremarkable. The situations below are the ones where it stops being a good idea, and none of them are a comment on how capable you are.

Situations, not conditions

  • Anywhere a moment of lightheadedness would be dangerous. Driving, cycling, swimming, a bath, a ladder, a staircase, standing at a train platform. Held breath and long exhales can make some people briefly woozy. Practice sitting on the floor or in a chair.
  • During a balance pose. Save box breathing for seated practice. In Tree Pose, use even breath and nothing more elaborate.
  • On a full stomach. Wait an hour or two after a large meal. Everything here is more comfortable when the diaphragm has somewhere to go.
  • While congested. A cold, sinus infection or blocked nose makes nasal patterns a struggle and alternate nostril breathing close to impossible. Skip a few days.
  • When counting is making you tense. If tracking numbers is winding you up rather than settling you, stop counting. Watching the breath without steering it is a complete practice on its own.

Signs to stop mid-practice

Return to ordinary, unmanaged breathing the moment any of these show up, and stay sitting until they pass. None of them mean you did something wrong; they mean the pattern was longer or stronger than today could take.

  • Dizzy or lightheaded Stop, open your eyes, breathe normally, stay down for a minute.
  • Tingling in the hands, lips or face Stop. This usually means you have been breathing more than your body asked for.
  • Anxious or short of breath Stop counting and let the breath be whatever it wants to be.
  • Chest pain, or vision that changes Stop and seek medical attention rather than trying again.

Two minutes, no setup

A two-minute version for a bad day

On the days when sitting down for five quiet minutes is not going to happen, this is the whole practice. It works in a parked car, in a stairwell, at a kitchen counter or on the edge of a bed, and it needs nothing but a seat.

  1. Twenty seconds: land

    Sit down and put both feet flat on the floor. Let your shoulders drop away from your ears. Do not change your breathing yet. Just notice what it is currently doing, whether that is short, high in the chest, or held.

  2. Forty seconds: four even breaths

    Inhale 4, exhale 4, through the nose. Four rounds. You are not trying to relax. You are finding out what tempo four counts is for you right now, which on a difficult day is usually faster than you expect.

  3. Sixty seconds: six exhales of 4:6

    Inhale 4, exhale 6. Six rounds is roughly a minute. Ration the exhale from the first count rather than dumping it and squeezing the end. If six is a strain, use five.

  4. The last breath: stop managing it

    Let one breath happen without a count attached, then stand up and get on with your day. The point is not to feel different. The point is that the practice stayed unbroken.

  • Two minutes counts A short practice done today beats a long one postponed to a better week.
  • No props, no floor A chair, a bench or a car seat is enough. Keep both feet down.
  • Skip the holds Bad days are not the time to add box breathing. Even breath and extended exhale only.

Building a breathing practice over a month

Three to five minutes on most days, attached to a point in your day that already exists, does more than twenty minutes once a week. A first month that works:

  • Weeks one and two: even breath, three minutes, sitting on a chair. Nothing added.
  • Week three: extended exhale, four minutes, lying down with the knees supported.
  • Week four: box breathing, two minutes only, seated, straight after the extended exhale.

Ocean breath belongs with movement rather than on its own, so fold it into the morning sequence once the sound comes easily. Alternate nostril breathing is worth adding in month two, not month one.

What this page does not claim

A great many claims get made for breathing techniques. None of them are made here. What can be said plainly is this: these patterns give you something exact and repeatable to put your attention on, they make an uneven breath obvious while you are in a pose, and a lot of people find them a steady way to open or close a practice.

That is the entire claim. If the physiology interests you, take the question to a qualified healthcare professional rather than to a yoga website.
