Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana  (AH-doh MOO-kah shvah-NAH-suh-nuh)

Hands down, feet down, hips high, spine long: an upside-down V. Almost everyone recognizes it and almost everyone hurries it. In a flowing class you may pass through this shape fifteen times in an hour, so the four minutes it takes to read the set-up properly are worth spending once.

The whole pose

Set-up, cues and every version

Adho Mukha Śvānāsana

  • Beginner
  • Shoulders
  • Hamstrings
  • No prop required
Difficulty
Beginner, but the load through the wrists is real. Learn the wall version first.
Breath pattern
Even. Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4 counts, through the nose the whole time.
Suggested hold
5 to 8 breaths, which lands somewhere around 30 to 45 seconds
What moves
Flexion at the shoulder under load, length through the spine, range at the ankle and calf, length in the hamstrings
Props that help
Two blocks, a wall, a sturdy chair, a folded blanket rolled under the heels
Where it appears
10-Minute Morning, Gentle Full Practice
Entry steps
  • Start on hands and knees, hands directly under the shoulders, knees directly under the hips.
  • Spread the fingers wide and turn both middle fingers to point straight at the top of the mat.
  • Press the whole hand into the floor, and pay particular attention to the pad at the base of the index finger. Most wrist discomfort in this shape comes from weight piling up in the heel of the hand.
  • Curl the toes under so the balls of both feet meet the mat.
  • Push the floor away and float the knees up. Leave them generously bent. This is not a temporary stage.
  • Aim your sit bones back and up until the spine feels long from the tailbone all the way to the crown of your head.
  • Now, and only now, start to straighten the knees, and only as far as the spine keeps that length. The moment your lower back begins to round, you have gone one degree too far. Back off.
Alignment cues
  • Length wins over straightness, every time. A long spine over bent knees is this pose. Straight legs under a rounded back is a different shape that does less for you.
  • Your heels are allowed to stay up. Where they land depends on calf length and the proportions of your bones. Plenty of people never put them down, and nothing about that is worth noticing.
  • Spiral the upper arms outward. Think of turning the inner elbows toward each other. That rotation opens space at the top of the shoulder joint instead of jamming the head of the arm bone into it.
  • Ears level with the upper arms. Let your head hang between them. Lifting it to look forward squeezes the back of your neck for the whole hold.
  • Draw the lowest ribs in. If they are pushing down toward the mat, the length is coming from the front of your ribcage rather than from your spine. Pull them gently back.
  • Share the weight front to back. Aim for roughly even pressure through hands and feet, rather than hanging the whole pose in your arms.
Common mistakes
  • Straightening the legs before lengthening the spine. The most common error here by a wide margin. It tucks the pelvis under, rounds the lower back, and converts a spine pose into a hamstring argument you will not win.
  • Walking the hands too far forward. The shape stretches out long and flat, the wrists take on more angle, and your shoulders end up carrying everything.
  • Clawing at the mat. Gripping fingers tell you the weight has collected in the heel of the hand. Spread the fingers instead and press the pads down.
  • Losing the breath. If your breathing stops or turns ragged, the pose is more than you have today. Bend the knees further, or come down to hands and knees.
  • Staying too long, too soon. Five clean breaths are worth more than forty seconds of shaking. Wrists and shoulders build tolerance over weeks, not sessions.
Accessible modifications

Each of these is the pose, not a smaller copy of it. Pick whichever one lets you keep an even breath and a long spine, and stay there as long as it is useful.

At a wall

Put both hands flat on a wall at roughly hip height, then walk your feet back until your torso is horizontal and your arms are straight. Same shoulder flexion, same spinal length, and almost none of the wrist angle. If your wrists object to the floor version, start here. It also teaches the shape more clearly, because you can see what your spine is doing.

Hands on a chair

Back a sturdy chair against a wall so it cannot slide, set your hands on the seat, walk your feet back and let your chest drop toward the floor. It sits neatly between the wall and the full pose, and it keeps your head above the level of your heart, which some days is the deciding factor.

Hands on blocks

Stand two blocks at their tallest setting, shoulder-width apart, and put one hand on each. Raising the floor closes the angle at the wrist and buys your spine a few inches of length it did not have. Flat, wide-faced blocks are far steadier here than rounded ones.

Forearms down

Drop to your forearms with the elbows directly under the shoulders and the hands shoulder-width apart, then lift the hips. This takes the wrists out of the conversation altogether and hands the work to your shoulders, which is a fair trade. A folded blanket under the forearms spares the elbows.

Progression

When eight even breaths feel unremarkable, with a long spine and hands that are not gripping, add a slow walk before you settle: bend one knee, press the opposite heel gently down, then change, five or six times each side. After that comes Three-Legged Dog, lifting one leg behind you while both hips stay level and both hands keep the same weight. How high the leg goes is beside the point. Level hips are the whole exercise.

Contraindications and cautions

Take the wall version, or take care, if you have wrist pain, a recent wrist or shoulder injury, carpal tunnel symptoms, uncontrolled high blood pressure, glaucoma or a recent eye procedure, a recent head or neck injury, or if you are in late pregnancy. Come out if you feel pinching inside the shoulder or wrist joint, numbness or tingling in the hands, or pressure building in your head.

Progression

How to know you are ready for the next step

There is a ladder here, and skipping a rung is what leads to sore wrists and a rounded back. Move up only when the rung you are on has been boring for about a week.

  1. Eight breaths at the wall, spine long

    Hands on the wall, torso horizontal, arms straight. You are ready to move on when you can hold eight even breaths and your lower back has not rounded at any point. If the last two breaths get short, you are not there yet, and there is no rush.

  2. Eight breaths with your hands on blocks

    Same eight breaths, now on the floor with two tall blocks under your hands. Check the wrists afterwards rather than during: soreness that shows up an hour later counts as too much. Stay at this rung for a fortnight if that keeps happening.

  3. Five breaths on the floor, knees generously bent

    Five is the number, not eight. Keep the knees clearly bent and watch that your fingers are not clawing. Come down to hands and knees the moment the breath turns ragged, not two breaths after.

  4. Eight breaths on the floor, legs finding their own length

    Now let the knees straighten as far as the spine allows, and no further. Heels may or may not arrive. This is where most people stay for years, and it is a perfectly good place to be.

  5. Three-Legged Dog, hips level

    Lift one leg behind you with both hip points still facing the floor and both hands carrying the same weight. Have someone look, or film ten seconds on your phone. Almost everybody lifts the hip long before they notice.

  • Breath Inhale 4 counts, exhale 4, through the nose.
  • Hold Five to eight breaths, then rest on hands and knees.
  • Prop A wall costs nothing and removes nearly all the wrist load.

Why it keeps coming back

A pose that works more like a corridor

This shape shows up so often that it stops behaving like a pose at all. In one flowing hour you might pass through it fifteen times. That is precisely why the set-up is worth your attention: a small habit repeated fifteen times a class, three times a week, is a habit you will be carrying for years.

What is actually happening: your shoulder joints move into flexion while holding weight, the muscles down the backs of your legs and calves take on length, and the muscles along your spine work to hold that length against gravity. Your wrists carry real load at an angle they rarely meet in daily life. That last part is why so many people quietly dread this pose, and exactly why the wall version exists.

Starting from nothing, give the first fortnight to the wall and the blocks before you put a single minute into the floor version. Your shoulders learn the same thing either way, and your wrists get the two weeks they need.

A long spine over bent knees is the pose. Straight legs under a rounded back is just a photograph of it.
