Class formats

What the six class labels actually mean

A studio schedule assumes you already know the difference between Hatha and Yin. Here is the plain version of all six: how long you stay in one shape, how much of the hour you spend moving, which props come out of the cupboard, and how much room the teacher leaves for a question.

Start with the part nobody says out loud. These words describe format, not quality, and they overlap heavily. A class called Hatha at one studio and a class called slow flow three blocks away can be the same hour under two different names. What a label does reliably tell you is the speed of the room and the length of the holds, and that covers most of what you need before you walk in.

The six below are ordered from the stillest to the most demanding on a first visit.

The word on the door predicts the pace of the room and very little else.

Restorative takes a handful of shapes and stays in them, with props carrying every part of you so that no muscle is asked to hold anything. Building one pose can take two or three minutes, and the teacher will often kneel down and adjust your bolster for you. Most of the hour is spent lying on the floor.

Nothing else on this list is close to it for gentleness, and nothing else surprises people more. Fifteen supported minutes is not the same as fifteen minutes on the sofa. It asks for a kind of patience that has no relationship to fitness.

If you have never set foot in a studio and want the least intimidating door, this is the one. Bring socks and a long-sleeved layer. Your body cools down fast once you stop moving.

Hatha

Poses per hour
Fifteen to twenty-five
Typical hold
3 to 8 breaths
Props
Blocks and straps, used as needed
Pace
Moderate, with pauses between poses
Good first class
Yes, especially if labeled beginner or fundamentals
What to expect
Instruction, setup time, poses held rather than passed through

Hatha is the loosest word on any schedule. Strictly, it covers nearly all physical yoga, which is exactly why studios reach for it when they want to name a general class that holds poses for a few breaths at a middling pace.

The hour goes like this: build a pose, stay in it for three to eight breaths while the teacher talks, come out, move to the next one. That gap between hearing a cue and having time to act on it is the whole advantage over faster rooms. Most of what sits in the pose library turns up in a Hatha class sooner or later.

For a first studio class, Hatha marked beginner, fundamentals or level one is the standard advice, and the standard advice is right.

Yin

Poses per hour
Twelve to eighteen, counting both sides
Typical hold
2 to 5 minutes
Props
Bolsters, blocks and blankets, used to support long holds
Pace
Very slow, mostly seated and lying
Good first class
Yes, but expect it to be mentally demanding
What to expect
Long stillness, muscles deliberately relaxed, quiet room

Yin settles into seated and lying shapes for two to five minutes each and asks you to let the muscles go slack rather than hold you there. The reasoning is that a long, mild load reaches the denser connective tissue around a joint differently from a short muscular one. Yin often uses its own pose names, so Pigeon becomes Sleeping Swan and Bound Angle becomes Butterfly.

Your body will find it easy. Your attention will not. Three minutes in a shape with no task attached is where most people notice how much of their practice was simply staying busy. Fidgeting somewhere in the second minute is normal and worth expecting.

Two practical notes. Dress warmer than feels necessary, because stillness cools you. And go in shallower than you could, because three minutes changes what a position feels like more than you would guess.

Iyengar

Poses per hour
Eight to fifteen
Typical hold
30 seconds to several minutes
Props
Central. Blocks, straps, chairs, bolsters, wall ropes, blankets
Pace
Deliberate, with long verbal instruction
Good first class
Yes, and the teaching is unusually precise
What to expect
Detailed alignment cues, repeated setups, teachers with long formal training

Iyengar takes its name from B. K. S. Iyengar, and it is built on precision and on props. Eight minutes can go by on one standing pose: set it up, come out, change a single thing, set it up again. Props here are not accommodations. They are standard equipment that everyone in the room uses, and most of the prop vocabulary the rest of yoga borrows started in this lineage.

Teacher training in this system runs far longer and far more formally than in most others, and you can hear that in the instruction. If you want to know what your back foot is doing and why it matters, this is the format that will tell you.

It makes an excellent first class for anyone curious about alignment, and it is the room most likely to give a specific, usable answer to a specific question about your own body.

Vinyasa

Poses per hour
Forty or more
Typical hold
1 to 5 breaths
Props
Blocks, occasionally. Less prop use than other formats
Pace
Continuous, timed to the breath
Good first class
Only if labeled slow flow, beginner flow or level one
What to expect
Movement almost the whole hour, repeated sequences, less individual instruction

Vinyasa strings poses into continuous sequences timed to the breath, one movement per inhale or exhale. The room moves for nearly the whole hour and usually repeats a sequence several times with small changes each round. Music is common. It is the most widely taught format in the United States right now.

Pace is the thing to watch. A quick Vinyasa class can pass you through Downward-Facing Dog fifteen times without anyone having a spare moment to mention your shoulders. That is not a fault in the format, but it does mean a first-timer can spend a full hour rehearsing one unhelpful habit.

If Vinyasa is all your neighborhood offers, pick the slowest thing on the schedule: slow flow, beginner flow or level one. Skip anything marked level two, power or advanced for now. Dropping into Child's Pose whenever you want is entirely normal, and the teacher will say so in the first minute.

Ashtanga

Poses per hour
Fixed sequence, roughly seventy in the primary series
Typical hold
5 breaths
Props
Rare
Pace
Vigorous and continuous
Good first class
Only in a led beginner class or a supervised Mysore room
What to expect
The same sequence every time, early mornings, a strong physical demand

Ashtanga runs one fixed sequence of poses in the same order every single time. Several series exist; almost everybody stays inside the primary series for years. You will meet it in two shapes: a led class where one teacher counts the whole room through together, and a Mysore room where each person works through their own portion at their own speed while a teacher circulates and gives individual attention.

It is physically hard and it repeats itself on purpose. The repetition is the method. Practicing the same sequence six mornings a week means every change you notice is measured against a fixed reference instead of a different class each time.

The Mysore room is, against most people's expectations, the friendlier door for a beginner. A teacher will hand you the first handful of poses and nothing else. A led primary series as your first-ever yoga class is not a good plan.

Decoding the schedule

How to read a class listing

A studio listing is usually four pieces of information crammed into one line: format, level, temperature and length. The level word tells you more about your next hour than the format word does, and it is the one most people skip over.

Words that appear on studio class listings and what each one means for a beginner
What the listing saysWhat it means in the room
Level one, fundamentals, foundationsPoses get built and explained. Look for this word before you look at anything else.
All levelsUsually means a general class with options offered out loud. It does not mean beginner.
GentleSlower, fewer standing poses, more floor. A reliable pick.
Slow flowVinyasa with the pace turned down and time to set up. The beginner-friendly end of flow.
Power, level two, advancedFast, strong, assumes you already know the shapes. Not a first class.
Heated or hotThe room is warmed, often between 85 and 105 °F. Bring two liters of water and a towel, and plan to leave the room if you need to.
CandlelitLow light, usually slower. Harder to see the teacher, so pick a spot near the front.
LedOne teacher counts the whole room through the same sequence together.
MysoreEveryone works at their own pace on their own portion while the teacher moves around. Beginners get a short version to start.
Restore, restorative, yoga nidraSupported and still. Bring warm layers and expect to lie down for most of it.
Community or donationLow-cost or pay-what-you-can, often taught by newer teachers. Class sizes can be large.
60 / 75 / 90Minutes. A 90-minute class usually means longer holds, not more poses.

  • Phone first One question, one minute: are props provided, or do I bring my own.
  • Arrive ten minutes early That window is when you tell the teacher this is your first class.

Matching the week you had

Which style suits which week

Picking a format by what your week has actually been like works better than picking one by ambition. Four common weeks, and the format that tends to fit each.

You sat at a desk for five days

Your hips and the front of your chest have been folded shut since Monday. Iyengar or Hatha gives you standing poses held long enough to feel where you are stuck, with a block under a hand so the spine can lengthen. A desk reset covers the same ground at home in eight minutes.

You are sore from running or lifting

Yin, with the caveat that sore is not the same as injured. Two to five minutes in a supported seated shape asks nothing of the muscles that are already complaining. Skip Yin entirely if something is sharp rather than tired.

You have slept badly all week

Restorative. Six shapes, propped from underneath, mostly lying down. It is the one format where turning up tired is an advantage rather than a problem to work around.

You have energy and nowhere to put it

Vinyasa or a led Ashtanga class, once the basic shapes are familiar enough that you are not learning them at speed. If they are not familiar yet, take a slow flow and let the pace build over a month.

Choose the format that fits the week you had, not the week you wish you had.

The short answer we give most often

The shortest answer

Which one to try first

If you want one sentence: find a class marked beginner, fundamentals or level one, in Hatha, Iyengar, Yin or Restorative. All four leave you enough time to hear an instruction and do something with it, which is what a first class is for.

If Vinyasa is the only option near you, take the slowest class on the schedule, put your mat near the back, and rest whenever you feel like it.
