01 / Three ways in

Pick the door that fits

Three doors, depending on your day

People land here needing one of three things. A place to start from zero. The detail on one particular pose. Or something to do with the ten minutes in front of them. Take whichever is true right now.

  1. You have never done this

    The beginner's guide runs about twelve minutes and answers the practical things first: what to wear, what is worth buying and what is not, how long to practice, what the Sanskrit means, and how to tell an ordinary stretch from something you should stop doing. It ends with a four-week plan.

  2. You want one pose in detail

    Thirty-eight poses, listed under both their English and Sanskrit names and filterable by difficulty, by the part of the body doing the work, and by the prop you have to hand. Six carry a full breakdown: entry steps, alignment cues, breath pattern, hold time, the mistakes people make, the modifications, and who should skip it.

  3. You have ten minutes and no plan

    Four sequences, from eight minutes in an office chair to a forty-minute gentle practice. Each one states its length, its difficulty and its prop list up front, then gives the poses in order with a hold time on every one. There is a timer on the page and a handout you can print and keep on the floor.

03 / Anatomy of a guide

One card, every pose

Look inside a pose guide

Every pose sits in the same card, in the same order, so the second guide you read takes half the time of the first. You always know where the hold time is. This is the top of the Child's Pose guide, unedited.

Child's Pose

Bālāsana  (BAH-lah-suh-nuh)

  • Beginner
  • Hips and spine
  • No prop required
Breath pattern
Inhale 4, exhale 8. Let the exhale be the longer half.
Suggested hold
1 to 3 minutes
Body area
Hips, lower back, ankles, shoulders
Props that help
Bolster, blanket, block, chair
Entry steps
  • Kneel with your shins on the floor and your big toes touching behind you.
  • Choose your knee width: together for a narrower shape, or as wide as the mat for more room at the front of the body.
  • Send your hips back toward your heels as far as they comfortably travel. They do not have to arrive.
  • Walk your hands forward and lower your forehead toward the mat, a stacked fist or a block.
  • Let the shoulders release and the jaw unclench.
Alignment cues
  • Forehead supported, not hanging. If it does not reach the floor, bring the floor up to it.
  • Sit bones traveling back rather than the chest pressing down.
  • Ankles long, tops of the feet on the mat, not tucked under.

The page carries on from here with the common mistakes, four modifications, one progression and a note on who should leave this pose alone. Read the whole guide.

04 / House style

Four rules we write to

What makes a guide here different

There is a great deal of yoga writing online and most of it is vague in the same four places. These are the rules that decide what goes on a Stillform page, and they are the reason the guides are longer than they look.

  • Numbers, not "a few breaths" Every hold has a time in seconds or minutes and every breath pattern has counts, so you know when the pose is over.
  • Props written first The chair, wall and block version is drafted before the full one, which is why it never reads like a consolation.
  • Mistakes from watching The common-mistakes list comes from teaching the pose to real beginners, not from a textbook's list of contraindications.
  • Nothing is claimed We say which joints move and which muscles hold you up. We do not say a pose fixes your back, your sleep or your mood.

If an instruction cannot be followed alone, on a floor, without a teacher to interpret it, it is not finished.

The one test every draft has to pass

That test is why a single pose guide can run to nine hundred words. It is also why the guides are plain: you should not have to decode a sentence while you are upside down. More on how the guides are made.

05 / A week at a time

About 50 minutes in total

How to use this site in one week

You do not need a plan to use this site, but here is one anyway. It costs under an hour spread across five days, it needs no props beyond a chair and a wall, and it introduces every part of the site in the order that makes sense.

  1. Monday and Thursday, ten minutes

    Run the ten-minute morning sequence twice this week. Same sequence both times. Repeating it is the point: the second run is where you stop reading every line and start noticing what your body is doing.

  2. Tuesday, eight minutes at your desk

    The desk reset runs from an office chair in whatever you are already wearing. It exists so that a busy week does not become a week off.

  3. Wednesday, read one pose properly

    Pick a shape that keeps turning up, open its guide, and read the alignment cues and common mistakes before you next practice it. Most people find one thing they had been doing for weeks without noticing.

  4. Friday, learn one word

    Five minutes in the glossary or on breathwork basics. Knowing what adho mukha or ujjayī means removes most of the reason a studio class feels like a language test.

  5. Saturday or Sunday, twenty minutes

    One longer practice on a day you are not watching the clock. The evening wind-down is entirely on the floor. Then start the week again. Nothing here gets better by being new.

Class names, decoded

What the word on the timetable means

Studio schedules are written as though everyone already knows. Here is each of the six common labels in a single sentence, so you can book a class without guessing what you are walking into.

Hatha
Poses held for several breaths at a moderate pace, with time to set each one up. The most common label for a general-level class.
Vinyasa
Poses linked into continuous sequences timed to the breath, so the class moves almost the whole time.
Yin
Mostly seated and lying shapes held for two to five minutes with the muscles deliberately relaxed.
Restorative
Six to eight shapes, each fully propped with bolsters and blankets, held for five to twenty minutes.
Iyengar
Precise alignment, long detailed instruction and heavy use of props. Expect to spend real time in a single pose.
Ashtanga
A fixed sequence practiced in the same order every time, at a vigorous pace, usually early in the morning.

Nothing behind a wall

Where the money comes from

Every pose guide, every sequence and every article is free to read. No login, no trial, no meter counting your articles. That is a decision about how the site is built, not an offer, and it is paid for in five ways that we would rather you knew about than guessed at.

  • Display advertising. Labeled slots, always below the content you came for. They never sit above it, never sit beside a button, and the space is reserved so the page does not jump when one loads.
  • An optional membership. $6 a month or $54 a year in US dollars. It clears the ads and adds downloadable sequence PDFs, saved practice collections and printable pose cards. It has never moved a free instruction behind a wall and it never will.
  • Affiliate commissions on mats, blocks, straps and bolsters. Buy through one of those links and we earn a percentage at no extra cost to you. The disclosure sits at the link, in the paragraph you are reading, not only down in the footer.
  • Paid digital products, such as the downloadable beginner program and the printable sequence bundles.
  • Sponsored articles and brand partnerships. Any piece a company paid for says sponsored in the first line, above the headline, before you have read a word of it.

The teacher directory also has a free listing tier and a paid featured placement that sits above it. Featured listings say featured on them.

Health and safety

What Stillform publishes is general movement education. It is not medical advice, and nothing on this site treats, prevents or manages any health condition.
